# T08.1: Why Normalize? Redundancy and Update Anomalies

> **Topic:** T08 — Normalization & Functional Dependencies | **Priority:** 🟠 High Priority | **Exam Frequency:** 4/7 finals (+ CT-03 Q2) (18 marks)
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 7 (§7.1, §7.1.1, §7.1.3, pp. 303–308) · Lecture Deck 06 (Slides 3–10) · Raidah Class Notes (p. 19)

## Why This Topic Matters

Normalization forms the bedrock of relational database design. Across semester finals, RUET examiners test this foundational subtopic frequently, awarding 18 final marks across four different years (2018, 2019, 2021, and 2023) plus CT-03. Exam questions follow two predictable patterns: either asking for theoretical definitions and examples of the three classic modification anomalies (insertion, deletion, and update anomalies), or providing an unnormalized data table and asking you to diagnose space inefficiency and predict future failure scenarios. Mastering this subtopic is essential because every subsequent normal form (1NF through BCNF) exists specifically to eliminate these exact anomalies.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Normalization:** "The method for designing a relational database is to use a process commonly known as normalization. The goal is to generate a set of relation schemas that allows us to store information without unnecessary redundancy, yet also allows us to retrieve information easily." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.1.3 (p. 308)

> **Lossless Decomposition:** "We say that the decomposition is a lossless decomposition if there is no loss of information by replacing $R$ with two relation schemas $R_1$ and $R_2$." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.1.2 (p. 307)

> **Update Anomaly:** *(Course definition)* "The same information must be updated in multiple rows. If one row is not updated, it leads to inconsistent data." — Lecture Deck 06, Slide 7, 8

> **Insertion Anomaly:** *(Course definition)* "We cannot insert information unless some unrelated information is also available." — Lecture Deck 06, Slide 7, 9

> **Deletion Anomaly:** *(Course definition)* "Deleting one piece of information accidentally removes another important piece of information." — Lecture Deck 06, Slide 7, 10

---

## The Pitfalls of Bad Relational Design

A poor database design tries to store facts about multiple independent real-world entities inside a single relation. For example, grouping students, courses, departments, and instructors into one wide table causes severe structural flaws:

1. **Unnecessary Data Redundancy:** The same real-world fact is recorded repeatedly across dozens or thousands of rows.
2. **Storage Wastage:** Large text strings such as department names, office locations, and course titles consume unnecessary disk storage and memory buffers.
3. **Modification Anomalies:** Basic everyday database operations (inserting a new record, updating an existing attribute, or deleting an outdated entry) produce unexpected side effects or inconsistent database states.

### Row-Level vs. Column-Level Duplication

Lecture Deck 06 distinguishes between two fundamental forms of duplication:

| Duplication Level | Nature of Problem | Mechanism to Resolve |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Row-Level Duplication** | An entire horizontal tuple across all columns is repeated identically in multiple rows. | **Primary Key** constraint (Entity Integrity enforces uniqueness across tuples). |
| **Column-Level Duplication** | A single attribute or group of attributes repeats the same value across distinct tuples because non-key entities are mixed into the table. | **Normalization** (Decomposing the relation into smaller, well-structured relations linked by foreign keys). |

---

## The Three Classic Modification Anomalies

When independent entities share a single wide table, everyday changes to the database trigger three classic anomalies.

### 1. Update Anomaly

An update anomaly happens when the same real-world fact is duplicated across many rows, so changing that single fact forces you to update every row where it appears.

- **What Goes Wrong:** If an `UPDATE` query modifies some rows but misses others (because of a crash, a partial filter, or a manual edit), the table ends up contradicting itself.
- **Example:** If an instructor's office phone number is stored inside every student enrollment row, changing that phone number requires updating dozens of rows. If even one row is missed, the same instructor has two conflicting phone numbers in the database.

### 2. Insertion Anomaly

An insertion anomaly happens when you cannot insert a valid, independent fact into the database because you are missing unrelated data that is required by the table's primary key.

- **What Goes Wrong:** Entity Integrity states that no part of a primary key can be `NULL`. When two different entities are combined into one table, one entity's primary key blocks you from recording the other entity on its own.
- **Example:** In an enrollment table with primary key `(Student_ID, Course_ID)`, you cannot add a brand-new course and its instructor to the table until at least one student enrolls in it, because `Student_ID` cannot be `NULL`.

### 3. Deletion Anomaly

A deletion anomaly happens when deleting a row to remove one fact accidentally wipes out a completely separate fact that happened to live only in that row.

- **What Goes Wrong:** You lose valuable independent information as an unintended side effect of a routine delete.
- **Example:** If only one student is enrolled in an elective course and they drop it, deleting their enrollment row also deletes the last copy of the course's title, syllabus, and instructor details from the database.

## Step-by-Step Walkthrough: How One Wide Table Causes All Three Anomalies

To see how redundancy and anomalies arise in practice, let's look at an unnormalized hospital table `PATIENT_DOCTOR_WARD` that mixes patient visits with doctor and ward details:

| Patient_ID | Patient_Name | Doctor_ID | Doctor_Name | Ward_No | Ward_Phone |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| P01 | Nabil | D10 | Dr. Karim | W-3 | 01711-000111 |
| P02 | Sumaiya | D10 | Dr. Karim | W-3 | 01711-000111 |
| P03 | Tanvir | D20 | Dr. Ruma | W-5 | 01811-222333 |

Here, the primary key is `Patient_ID`, because each row records a patient currently admitted under a doctor. However, notice that doctor and ward facts depend on `Doctor_ID` ($\text{Doctor\_ID} \to \text{Doctor\_Name}, \text{Ward\_No}, \text{Ward\_Phone}$), not on `Patient_ID`. That mismatch creates four problems:

1. **Data Redundancy:** Dr. Karim's name, ward number (`W-3`), and ward phone (`01711-000111`) are repeated for every patient he treats (rows 1 and 2). If he treats 80 patients, those strings are stored 80 times.
2. **Update Anomaly:** If Ward `W-3` gets a new phone number, the database must update every row where `Ward_No = 'W-3'`. If row 1 is updated but row 2 is missed, Ward `W-3` shows two different phone numbers.
3. **Insertion Anomaly:** Suppose the hospital hires a new specialist, `D30: Dr. Farhana` in Ward `W-7`, who has not been assigned any patients yet. Because `Patient_ID` is the primary key and cannot be `NULL`, we cannot insert Dr. Farhana's record into the table without inventing a fake patient!
4. **Deletion Anomaly:** Right now, `P03` (Tanvir) is the only patient assigned to `D20` (Dr. Ruma in Ward `W-5`). When Tanvir recovers and is discharged, deleting row `P03` also erases all record of Dr. Ruma and Ward `W-5` from the database.

### The Remedy: Lossless Decomposition
Normalization fixes all three anomalies at once by splitting `PATIENT_DOCTOR_WARD` into two clean tables linked by a foreign key:
1. `DOCTOR_WARD(Doctor_ID, Doctor_Name, Ward_No, Ward_Phone)` with primary key `Doctor_ID`
2. `PATIENT(Patient_ID, Patient_Name, Doctor_ID)` with primary key `Patient_ID` and foreign key `Doctor_ID` referencing `DOCTOR_WARD`

Now each doctor's ward phone is stored in just one row, new doctors can be added into `DOCTOR_WARD` with zero patients, and discharging Tanvir from `PATIENT` leaves Dr. Ruma's record in `DOCTOR_WARD` untouched.

## How to Diagnose Anomalies on Exam Tables (4-Step Recipe)

Whenever a RUET exam gives you a sample data table and asks whether it has redundancy or what problems it will cause in the future (like in 2019 Q.6(a) and 2021 Q.6(c) below), follow this 4-step checklist:

1. **Check for Multi-Valued Cells (1NF Violation):** Look at every cell first. If a single cell holds multiple values (like two course codes stacked in one box), point out that it violates First Normal Form (1NF), wastes space, and requires awkward string parsing during updates or deletes.
2. **Spot Repeated Non-Key Columns (Column-Level Redundancy):** Even if the primary key column (`st_id` or `Roll`) has unique values, scan across the non-key columns. If attributes like `Course`, `Fee`, `Session`, or `Dept` repeat the exact same values down the table, state clearly that column-level redundancy exists because a non-key determinant ($\text{Course} \to \text{Session}, \text{Fee}, \text{Dept}$) is mixed into the table.
3. **Construct the Update & Insertion Anomalies:** Pick a repeated column (like `Fee`) and explain what happens if it changes for one row but not the others (**update anomaly**). Then explain why a new entity (like a new course with zero students) cannot be inserted because the primary key (`st_id`) cannot be `NULL` (**insertion anomaly**).
4. **Find the "Last Remaining Row" for the Deletion Anomaly:** Look for an entity that appears in only one row (like a student taking a single course, or a course where all enrolled students graduate) and explain what unrelated information gets wiped out when those rows are deleted (**deletion anomaly**).

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2018 Q.4(a) · Problems Without Normalization

> **Appeared:** 2018 Q.4(a) — (04 Marks)

**Question:**

What problem may occur if databases are used without normalization? Discuss with proper example.

**Full Answer:**

Using a database system without normalization leads to four fundamental problems:

1. **Data Redundancy:** Multiple copies of the same real-world information are stored repeatedly across different rows. This wastes storage space and increases disk I/O costs.
2. **Update Anomaly:** Because data is duplicated, modifying a single real-world fact requires finding and updating every occurrence. If any row is overlooked or if an update fails halfway, different records present conflicting values, causing data inconsistency.
3. **Insertion Anomaly:** Independent facts cannot be inserted into the database unless unrelated data is also available. A new entity cannot be added without providing a primary key value for an entity that may not yet exist.
4. **Deletion Anomaly:** Deleting a record to remove one set of facts inadvertently deletes unrelated, valuable facts that exist only within that record.

#### Concrete Example

Consider an unnormalized table storing student enrollment and instructor assignments:

| Student_ID | Student_Name | Course | Instructor | Instructor_Phone |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| S01 | Riad | DBMS | Dr. Hasan | 01711... |
| S01 | Riad | AI | Dr. Abdullah | 01811... |
| S02 | Zoha | DBMS | Dr. Hasan | 01711... |

Primary Key: `(Student_ID, Course)`

- **Redundancy:** Dr. Hasan's name and phone number are stored multiple times for every student enrolled in DBMS.
- **Update Anomaly:** If Dr. Hasan changes his mobile phone number, all tuples where he teaches DBMS must be updated. If row 1 is updated but row 3 is missed, the database shows two conflicting phone numbers for the same instructor.
- **Insertion Anomaly:** If the university introduces a new course "Machine Learning" taught by Dr. Kamal, this information cannot be stored if no student has enrolled yet. Because `Student_ID` is part of the primary key, it cannot be `NULL` under Entity Integrity.
- **Deletion Anomaly:** If student `S01` drops the course "AI" and row 2 is deleted, all information about Dr. Abdullah and the fact that AI is taught at the university is permanently erased.

### 🎯 GQ2 · 2019 Q.6(a) · Unnormalized Student Table with Multi-Valued Attributes

> **Appeared:** 2019 Q.6(a) — (06 Marks)

**Question:**

Consider the following data tables.

**Student**  
<table border="1" cellpadding="4" style="border-collapse: collapse; text-align: center; margin-top: 6px; margin-bottom: 6px;"><thead><tr><th>Roll</th><th>Name</th><th>Dept</th><th>Sub Code</th><th>Sub Grade</th><th>Reg Sub Code</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1710001</td><td>Mr. A</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2103</td><td>A+</td><td>ECE-2103<br>ECE-2107</td></tr><tr><td>1710002</td><td>Mr. B</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2107</td><td>A</td><td>ECE-2103<br>ECE-2107</td></tr><tr><td>1710003</td><td>Mr. C</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2103</td><td>A-</td><td>ECE-2103</td></tr><tr><td>1710004</td><td>Mr. D</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2107</td><td>A+</td><td>ECE-2107</td></tr><tr><td>1710001</td><td>Mr. A</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2107</td><td>A-</td><td>ECE-2103<br>ECE-2107</td></tr><tr><td>1710002</td><td>Mr. B</td><td>ECE</td><td>ECE-2103</td><td>A+</td><td>ECE-2103<br>ECE-2107</td></tr></tbody></table>  
Now,  
(i) Carefully notice the entries and point out your opinion regarding space efficiency.  
(ii) What will happen if you update any particular information?  
(iii) What will happen if you are asked to delete 'ECE-2107' from the registered course list preserving all other information?

**Full Answer:**

#### (i) Opinion Regarding Space Efficiency

The given table is highly space-inefficient for three specific structural reasons:

1. **Repetitive Demographic Information:** The attributes `Name` ("Mr. A", "Mr. B") and `Dept` ("ECE") are repeated across multiple rows for the same `Roll`. For example, student 1710001 has two separate rows, repeating his name and department.
2. **Multi-Valued Redundant List in `Reg Sub Code`:** The column `Reg Sub Code` stores composite lists containing multiple course codes (`ECE-2103, ECE-2107`). Storing this multi-valued text string in every row of a student multiplies storage requirements.
3. **Violation of First Normal Form (1NF):** Relational databases require attribute domains to be atomic. Storing multiple values inside a single table cell violates 1NF, requiring variable-length text fields and inefficient parsing during search queries.

#### (ii) Consequences of Updating Information

Updating any student or course attribute triggers an **update anomaly**:

- **Student Attribute Updates:** If student 1710001 changes his name, department, or contact details, every single row corresponding to Roll `1710001` must be updated.
- **Registration Updates:** If a student registers for a new course or drops a course, the multi-valued string in `Reg Sub Code` must be updated across all rows for that student.
- **Risk of Data Inconsistency:** If an update query updates row 1 for Roll `1710001` but fails to update row 5, the student will have two different names or conflicting department affiliations in the database.

#### (iii) Consequences of Deleting 'ECE-2107' from the Registered Course List

If instructed to delete 'ECE-2107' from the registered course list while preserving all other data:

1. **For students with multiple registered courses (e.g., 1710001, 1710002):**
   The database cannot simply delete rows because doing so would delete their grades for `ECE-2103`. Instead, the system must perform string manipulation on `Reg Sub Code` in all their rows, parsing the cell and stripping the substring `'ECE-2107'`. This operation is computationally inefficient and prone to syntax errors.
2. **For student 1710004 (Mr. D):**
   His only registered course is `ECE-2107`. If `ECE-2107` is removed:
   - Setting `Reg Sub Code` to `NULL` leaves an incomplete record.
   - If the business rule or primary key requires a valid registered course, deleting the row causes a **deletion anomaly**: it permanently deletes Mr. D's roll number, name, department, and his grade (`A+`) from the database.

Consider the following table:

Is there any data redundancy in the above table? What kinds of problems/ anomalities it will produce for this table in future?

*(Note: "problems/ anomalities" printed verbatim from original question paper)*

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Presence of Data Redundancy

**Yes, substantial data redundancy exists in the table.**

Although each tuple has a unique primary key `st_id`, the values for `Course` ("DBMS"), `Session` ("2022"), `Fee` ("90k"), and `Dept` ("CS") are duplicated identically across all five tuples. In relational schema design, `Fee`, `Session`, and `Dept` functionally depend on `Course`, not on `st_id`. Mixing course-level attributes into individual student records produces column-level redundancy.

#### 2. Future Problems and Anomalies

1. **Storage Inefficiency:**
   Storing identical character strings ("DBMS", "2022", "90k", "CS") for every single student enrolled in the course wastes disk storage and buffer pool memory.
2. **Update Anomaly:**
   If the department raises the course fee for DBMS from 90k to 95k, the database system must update every single student row. Because `st_id` is the primary key, it cannot accept a `NULL` value under the Entity Integrity rule.
4. **Deletion Anomaly:**
   If all five students (101 through 105) complete their degree, drop the course, or cancel enrollment, deleting their records removes all five rows. This completely destroys all record of the DBMS course, its fee structure, and its department affiliation.

### 🎯 GQ4 · 2023 Q.2(b) · Anomalies Normalization Aims to Eliminate

> **Appeared:** 2023 Q.2(b) — (04 Marks)

**Question:**

What are the anomalies that normalization aims to eliminate? Provide examples of insertion, deletion and update anomalies.

**Full Answer:**

Normalization aims to eliminate three modification anomalies caused by unnecessary data redundancy and undesirable functional dependencies:

1. **Insertion Anomaly**
2. **Deletion Anomaly**
3. **Update Anomaly**

#### Illustrative Example: The `EMP_DEPT` Relation

Consider an unnormalized relation combining employee and department records into a single schema:

| Emp_ID | Emp_Name | Dept_ID | Dept_Name | Location |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| E01 | Alice | D10 | IT | Building A |
| E02 | Bob | D20 | HR | Building B |
| E03 | Charlie | D10 | IT | Building A |

**Primary Key:** `Emp_ID`

#### 1. Insertion Anomaly
- **Definition:** An insertion anomaly occurs when a new record cannot be inserted into a relation without providing dummy or irrelevant data for an unrelated entity.
- **Example:** The enterprise establishes a new department `D30` (Finance, located in Building C). Because no employee has been hired yet, `Emp_ID` is unavailable. Because `Emp_ID` serves as the primary key, it cannot be `NULL` due to the Entity Integrity constraint. Therefore, department `D30` cannot be stored in the database.

#### 2. Deletion Anomaly
- **Definition:** A deletion anomaly occurs when the deletion of data representing one real-world entity unintentionally causes the deletion of data representing a completely different real-world entity.
- **Example:** Employee `E02` (Bob) is currently the only employee working in the HR department (`D20`). If Bob resigns and his record is deleted, the entire record of department `D20` (its name "HR" and location "Building B") is permanently lost.

#### 3. Update Anomaly
- **Definition:** An update anomaly occurs when modifying a single real-world fact requires updating redundant data values across multiple records.
- **Example:** The IT department (`D10`) moves from Building A to Building D. Because department location is duplicated across every employee in that department, both rows `E01` and `E03` must be modified. If the system updates `E01` but fails to update `E03`, department `D10` has two conflicting locations in the database.

### 🎯 GQ5 · CT-03 Q.02 · Problems Caused by an Unnormalized Relation

> **Appeared:** CT-03 Q.02 — (03 Marks)

**Question:**

Mention the problems caused by an unnormalized relation.

**Full Answer:**

An unnormalized relation causes four major problems:

1. **Data Redundancy:** Facts about independent entities are stored repeatedly across multiple records, wasting physical storage space and degrading query performance.
2. **Update Anomaly:** Modifying a duplicated piece of information requires updating all occurrences across the table. Missing a single tuple produces contradictory, inconsistent data.
3. **Insertion Anomaly:** Independent information cannot be added to the database without also supplying unrelated information or violating primary key constraints (Entity Integrity forbidding `NULL` keys).
4. **Deletion Anomaly:** Deleting a record to remove one entity inadvertently erases unrelated, essential information that exists only within that record.

## 📖 High-Yield Exercises from the Book

### 🎯 HYE-01 · Exercise 7.23 · Repetition of Information and Inability to Represent Information

> **Source:** Silberschatz 7e · Chapter 7 (Exercise 7.23)  
> **Exam Relevance:** High-Probability Foundational Design Theory Drill  
> **Key Tested Concept:** Repetition of information and inability to represent information as the two fundamental hallmarks of bad relational design.

**Problem Statement:**
> Explain what is meant by **repetition of information** and **inability to represent information**. Explain why each of these properties may indicate a bad relational-database design.

**Model Exam-Style Solution:**

1. **Repetition of Information:**
   - **Meaning:** Repetition of information occurs when the same real-world fact is stored redundantly across multiple tuples in a relation. In an unnormalized schema—such as the combined schema $in\_dep(\text{ID}, \text{name}, \text{salary}, \text{dept\_name}, \text{building}, \text{budget})$—the building and budget of a department are replicated for every instructor employed in that department.
   - **Why it indicates a bad design:**
     - **Storage Waste:** Redundant data unnecessarily consumes secondary storage and fills memory buffer pools.
     - **Update Inconsistency (Update Anomaly):** When a department's budget or building changes, every single tuple containing that department must be tracked down and updated. If system failure or query omission causes only some tuples to update while others retain the old value, the database falls into an inconsistent state with mutually contradictory facts.

2. **Inability to Represent Information:**
   - **Meaning:** Inability to represent information occurs when certain independent real-world facts cannot be stored in the relation without simultaneously asserting unrelated facts, or without resorting to artificial `NULL` values.
   - **Why it indicates a bad design:**
     - **Insertion Anomaly:** If a university establishes a new department (e.g., "Cybersecurity" in "Building D" with budget 500,000) but has not yet hired any instructor, this department cannot be inserted into the $in\_dep$ schema. The primary key includes instructor $\text{ID}$, which cannot be `NULL` due to the **Entity Integrity constraint**. Thus, legitimate organizational data cannot be recorded.
     - **Deletion Anomaly:** If an existing department has only one instructor and that instructor resigns, deleting that instructor tuple unintentionally erases the department's entire existence, including its building and budget.
     - **Null Value Hazards:** Attempting to represent standalone entities by placing `NULL` into foreign/candidate keys creates severe complications in SQL query evaluation (three-valued logic, aggregation anomalies with `AVG`/`COUNT`, and broken referential integrity).

3. **Engineering Remedy (Decomposition):**
   To resolve both design flaws, the universal relation $in\_dep$ must be decomposed into two independent, normalized schemas:
   $$
   \text{instructor}(\underline{\text{ID}}, \text{name}, \text{dept\_name}, \text{salary})
   $$
   $$
   \text{department}(\underline{\text{dept\_name}}, \text{building}, \text{budget})
   $$
   - Department facts are stored exactly once in $department$, eliminating **repetition of information**.
   - Departments can exist before any instructor is hired, eliminating the **inability to represent information**. $\blacksquare$

**💡 RUET Exam Takeaway:**
When examiners ask about the "criteria of a good relational design" or "flaws of unnormalized tables", frame your answer around these two core textbook pillars: **Repetition of Information** (driving update anomalies and storage overhead) and **Inability to Represent Information** (driving insertion/deletion anomalies and NULL complications). Always illustrate with the classic Silberschatz $in\_dep \to (instructor, department)$ decomposition.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Always Draw a Small 3-Row Table:** Even if an exam question does not provide data (like 2018 Q.4(a) or 2023 Q.2(b)), draw a simple 3-row, 5-column table (`STUDENT_COURSE_INSTRUCTOR` or `EMP_DEPT`). Showing all three anomalies on the same data table guarantees full marks.
2. **Explain the Root Cause of Insertion Failures:** When explaining the insertion anomaly, state explicitly that primary key columns cannot contain `NULL` due to the **Entity Integrity constraint**. Examiners look for this exact technical term.
3. **Do Not Confuse Row Duplication with Column Duplication:** In 2021 Q.6(c), students often mistakenly claim there is no redundancy because every row has a unique student ID (`st_id`). Clearly explain that redundancy exists across the non-key attributes (`Course`, `Session`, `Fee`, `Dept`).
4. **Identify Mr. D in 2019 Q.6(a):** When answering part (iii) of 2019 Q.6(a), always highlight student `1710004` (Mr. D). Because `ECE-2107` is his only registered course, removing it causes a severe deletion anomaly that erases his entire academic record.
