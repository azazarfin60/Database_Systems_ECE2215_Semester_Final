# T02.3: Mapping Cardinality and Participation Constraints

> **Topic:** T02 — E-R & EER Modeling | **Priority:** 🟡 Medium | **Exam Frequency:** 2/7 finals (8 marks)  
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 6 (§6.4, pp. 252–258) · Lecture Deck 05 (Slides 20–28) · Classnote by Raidah (pp. 18–19) · Handnote by Fahim (pp. 28–30)

## Why This Topic Matters

Mapping cardinality and participation constraints capture the real-world business rules of a database. They answer two practical questions: *How many entities on one side can link to an entity on the other side?* and *Can an entity exist without participating in the relationship at all?* In RUET semester finals, examiners regularly ask you to define the four cardinality ratios and show how a many-to-many ($M:N$) relationship is converted into relational tables. Getting these rules right is also the key to drawing clean E-R diagrams and avoiding redundant tables during schema reduction.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Mapping Cardinalities:** "Mapping cardinalities, or cardinality ratios, express the number of entities to which another entity can be associated via a relationship set."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §6.4 (p. 252)

> **Total Participation:** "The participation of an entity set $E$ in a relationship set $R$ is said to be total if every entity in $E$ must participate in at least one relationship in $R$."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §6.4 (p. 255)

> **Partial Participation:** "If it is possible that some entities in $E$ do not participate in relationships in $R$, the participation of entity set $E$ in relationship $R$ is said to be partial."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §6.4 (p. 255)

## 1. Mapping Cardinality Ratios (Binary Relationships)

**Intuitive Rule for Silberschatz Arrows:** In Silberschatz's E-R notation, an **arrowhead ($\rightarrow$)** always points to the **"at most one"** side, while a **plain undirected line ($-$)** means **"many" (zero or more)**.

For any binary relationship set $R$ connecting entity sets $A$ and $B$, the mapping cardinality falls into one of four categories:

```text
  1:1 (One-to-One)         1:N (One-to-Many)        N:1 (Many-to-One)        M:N (Many-to-Many)
  A ────► R ◄──── B        A ────── R ◄──── B       A ────► R ────── B       A ────── R ────── B
 (At most 1 on both)      (A has many, B has 1)    (A has 1, B has many)    (Many on both sides)
```

### 1. One-to-One (1:1)
- **Rule:** Each entity in $A$ is linked to at most one entity in $B$, and each entity in $B$ is linked to at most one entity in $A$.
- **Chen Notation:** Directed arrows ($\rightarrow$) point from the relationship diamond to **both** $A$ and $B$.
- **Real-World Example:** A `department` has at most one department head (`dept_head`), and an `instructor` can head at most one department.

### 2. One-to-Many (1:N)
- **Rule:** Each entity in $A$ can be linked to any number (zero or more) of entities in $B$, while each entity in $B$ is linked to at most one entity in $A$.
- **Chen Notation:** A directed arrow ($\rightarrow$) points to $A$ (the "one" side), and an undirected line connects to $B$ (the "many" side).
- **Real-World Example:** One `instructor` can advise many students, but each `student` has at most one faculty advisor.

### 3. Many-to-One (N:1)
- **Rule:** Each entity in $A$ is linked to at most one entity in $B$, while each entity in $B$ can be linked to any number (zero or more) of entities in $A$.
- **Chen Notation:** An undirected line connects to $A$ (the "many" side), and a directed arrow ($\rightarrow$) points to $B$ (the "one" side).
- **Real-World Example:** Many `student` entities belong to a single academic `department` (`stud_dept`), whereas each student is enrolled in only one department.

### 4. Many-to-Many (M:N)
- **Rule:** Each entity in $A$ can be linked to zero or more entities in $B$, and each entity in $B$ can be linked to zero or more entities in $A$.
- **Chen Notation:** Plain undirected lines connect the relationship diamond to both $A$ and $B$ (no arrows).
- **Real-World Example:** A `student` can register for multiple courses, and a `course` can enroll multiple students (`Takes`).

## Cardinality Comparison Table

| Cardinality Ratio | Entity $A$ associates with | Entity $B$ associates with | Arrow Placement in E-R | Primary Key Rule for Relationship |
| :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1:1** | At most 1 in $B$ | At most 1 in $A$ | Arrows to both $A$ and $B$ | Primary key of either $A$ or $B$ |
| **1:N** | Zero or more in $B$ | At most 1 in $A$ | Arrow to $A$ ("one" side) | Primary key of $B$ (the "Many" side) |
| **N:1** | At most 1 in $B$ | Zero or more in $A$ | Arrow to $B$ ("one" side) | Primary key of $A$ (the "Many" side) |
| **M:N** | Zero or more in $B$ | Zero or more in $A$ | No arrows (undirected lines) | Composite key: $\text{PK}(A) \cup \text{PK}(B)$ |

## 2. Participation Constraints

While mapping cardinality sets the *maximum* number of links, participation constraints tell us the *minimum*: does every single entity in the set have to participate in the relationship, or is it optional?

### Total Participation (Existence Dependency)
- Every entity in the entity set **must** participate in at least one relationship instance.
- Because an entity cannot exist in the database without having this link, total participation is also called **existence dependency**.
- **Chen E-R Notation:** Drawn as a **double line** between the entity box and the relationship diamond.
- **Example:** If university rules say every student must have an assigned advisor, then `student` has total participation in `advisor`.

### Partial Participation
- Some entities in the entity set can exist on their own without participating in any relationship instance.
- **Chen E-R Notation:** Drawn as a **single line** between the entity box and the relationship diamond.
- **Example:** Some professors only do research and do not advise any students, so `instructor` has partial participation in `advisor`.

## 3. Min..Max Constraint Notation ($l..h$)

**Intuitive Idea:** Instead of combining arrows and double lines, we can write a pair of numbers $l..h$ directly on an entity's connecting line to state both participation and cardinality at once. Just read $l..h$ on the line touching entity set $E$ as: *"Each individual entity in $E$ appears in this relationship at least $l$ times and at most $h$ times."*

- $l$ is the **minimum** number of times each entity must participate:
  - $l = 1$ means **total participation** (equivalent to a double line).
  - $l = 0$ means **partial participation** (equivalent to a single line).
- $h$ is the **maximum** number of times each entity can participate:
  - $h = 1$ means the entity links to **at most one** partner.
  - $h = *$ means **no upper limit** (many).

### Common $l..h$ Labels
- **$0..*$:** Partial participation, unlimited links.
- **$1..1$:** Total participation, exactly one link.
- **$1..*$:** Total participation, one or more links.
- **$0..1$:** Partial participation, at most one link.

> [!WARNING] The Edge Reversal Trap
> In Silberschatz notation, the label $l..h$ on the line between entity $E$ and relationship $R$ counts how many times **each entity in $E$** participates in $R$.  
> For example, putting $1..1$ on the `student` side and $0..*$ on the `instructor` side of `advisor` means each student has **exactly one** advisor (so the arrow points to `instructor`), while each instructor advises **zero or more** students!

## 4. Cardinality Constraints on Ternary Relationships

In a ternary (or higher-degree) relationship diamond, Silberschatz allows **at most one arrow** leaving the diamond:
- An arrow pointing to entity set $C$ means that any specific pair of entities $(a, b)$ from $A \times B$ can be linked to **at most one** entity in $C$.
- Drawing two or more arrows on a ternary diamond is not allowed because literatures interpret multiple arrows in two conflicting ways, making the diagram ambiguous.

## 5. Converting a Many-to-Many (M:N) Relationship into Relational Tables

When two entity sets have an $M:N$ relationship, you cannot simply drop a foreign key column into one of the entity tables. If you tried to put `course_id` inside the `student` table, a student taking five courses would either need five repeating columns or a comma-separated list in one cell, which violates First Normal Form (1NF).

### Conversion Algorithm (Step-by-Step)

1. **Create the Two Entity Tables:** Keep a separate table for each participating entity set with its own primary key and attributes.
2. **Create a Junction (Bridge) Table:** Create a third table dedicated to the $M:N$ relationship set.
3. **Import Both Primary Keys as Foreign Keys:** Add the primary keys of both participating entity sets as columns in the junction table.
4. **Include Descriptive Attributes:** Add any descriptive attributes from the relationship diamond into the junction table.
5. **Define the Composite Primary Key:** Set the primary key of the junction table to the combination (union) of both foreign keys:
   $$
   \text{PK}(\text{Junction Table}) = \text{PK}(\text{Entity } 1) \cup \text{PK}(\text{Entity } 2)
   $$

## Worked Example: Doctor Treats Patient (Mini-Trace)

Suppose a hospital has an $M:N$ relationship `treats` between `doctor` and `patient`, with a descriptive attribute `diagnosis` recording the condition treated:
- **Entity Table 1:** $\text{doctor}(\underline{\text{doc\_id}}, \text{name}, \text{specialty})$
- **Entity Table 2:** $\text{patient}(\underline{\text{pat\_id}}, \text{name}, \text{phone})$
- **Junction Table:** $\text{treats}(\underline{\text{doc\_id}}, \underline{\text{pat\_id}}, \text{diagnosis})$, where `doc_id` references `doctor(doc_id)` and `pat_id` references `patient(pat_id)`.
- **Why the composite PK works:** Doctor `D1` can treat both patients `P1` and `P2`, and patient `P1` can be treated by both `D1` and `D2`. Neither `doc_id` nor `pat_id` is unique on its own in `treats`, but the pair $(\text{doc\_id}, \text{pat\_id})$ uniquely identifies every row.

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2018 Q.3(c) · Mapping Cardinality & M:N Conversion to Table

> **Appeared:** 2018 Q.3(c) — (4 marks)

> **Question:** What is mapping cardinality? How a many to many relationship can be converted into table. Explain with example.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Mapping Cardinality Definition
Mapping cardinality (or cardinality ratio) expresses the number of entities to which another entity can be associated via a relationship set. For binary relationships, the mapping cardinality takes one of four forms: $1:1$, $1:N$, $N:1$, or $M:N$.

#### 2. Conversion of a Many-to-Many (M:N) Relationship into Relational Tables
In a relational database, an $M:N$ relationship cannot be stored by placing a foreign key inside either entity table without repeating groups and violating First Normal Form (1NF). Instead, we convert an $M:N$ relationship into tables using five steps:
1. Keep the two participating entity sets as separate relational tables with their own primary keys and attributes.
2. Create a third **junction table** (relationship relation) to represent the $M:N$ relationship set.
3. Include the primary keys of both participating entity sets inside the junction table as foreign keys.
4. Add any descriptive attributes of the relationship set as columns in the junction table.
5. Set the primary key of the junction table to the composite union of both foreign keys:
   $$
   \text{PK}(\text{Junction}) = \text{PK}(E_1) \cup \text{PK}(E_2)
   $$

#### 3. Concrete Example (`student`, `course`, and `takes`)
Consider students enrolling in university courses:
- **Entity Set 1 (`STUDENT`):** Has primary key `student_id` and attributes `name`, `dept_name`, `tot_cred`.
- **Entity Set 2 (`COURSE`):** Has primary key `course_id` and attributes `title`, `dept_name`, `credits`.
- **Relationship (`Takes`):** An $M:N$ relationship with descriptive attribute `grade`.

**Resulting Relational Schemas:**
1. $\text{student}(\underline{\text{student\_id}}, \text{name}, \text{dept\_name}, \text{tot\_cred})$ (Primary Key: `student_id`)
2. $\text{course}(\underline{\text{course\_id}}, \text{title}, \text{dept\_name}, \text{credits})$ (Primary Key: `course_id`)
3. $\text{takes}(\underline{\text{student\_id}}, \underline{\text{course\_id}}, \text{grade})$ (Composite Primary Key: `(student_id, course_id)`)

```sql
CREATE TABLE takes (
    student_id   VARCHAR(10),
    course_id    VARCHAR(8),
    grade        VARCHAR(2),
    PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (student_id) REFERENCES student(student_id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
    FOREIGN KEY (course_id) REFERENCES course(course_id) ON DELETE CASCADE
);
```

**Verification Trace:** If student `2310001` takes `ECE 2215` (grade `A+`) and `ECE 2201` (grade `A`), and student `2310002` also takes `ECE 2215` (grade `A+`), the `takes` table stores three rows: `('2310001', 'ECE 2215', 'A+')`, `('2310001', 'ECE 2201', 'A')`, and `('2310002', 'ECE 2215', 'A+')`. Every row is uniquely identified by the composite key `(student_id, course_id)`.

### 🎯 GQ2 · 2020 Q.1(c) · Mapping Cardinalities & Four Types

> **Appeared:** 2020 Q.1(c) — (4 marks)

> **Question:** What is mapping cardinalities? Define different types of cardinalities.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Definition
Mapping cardinalities (or cardinality ratios) specify the number of entities to which another entity can be associated through a relationship set. They enforce structural business rules on database relationships.

#### 2. The Four Types of Binary Mapping Cardinalities

1. **One-to-One (1:1):**
   - **Definition:** An entity in entity set $A$ is associated with at most one entity in entity set $B$, and an entity in $B$ is associated with at most one entity in $A$.
   - **E-R Notation:** Drawn with directed arrows ($\rightarrow$) pointing from the relationship diamond to both $A$ and $B$.
   - **Example:** A department has at most one head instructor, and an instructor heads at most one department (`dept_head`).

2. **One-to-Many (1:N):**
   - **Definition:** An entity in $A$ is associated with any number (zero or more) of entities in $B$, while an entity in $B$ is associated with at most one entity in $A$.
   - **E-R Notation:** Drawn with a directed arrow ($\rightarrow$) pointing to $A$ (the "one" side) and an undirected line to $B$ (the "many" side).
   - **Example:** One instructor can advise many students, but each student has at most one advisor (`advisor`).

3. **Many-to-One (N:1):**
   - **Definition:** An entity in $A$ is associated with at most one entity in $B$, while an entity in $B$ can be associated with any number (zero or more) of entities in $A$.
   - **E-R Notation:** Drawn with an undirected line to $A$ (the "many" side) and a directed arrow ($\rightarrow$) pointing to $B$ (the "one" side).
   - **Example:** Many students belong to one academic department, while each student belongs to a single department (`stud_dept`).

4. **Many-to-Many (M:N):**
   - **Definition:** An entity in $A$ is associated with any number (zero or more) of entities in $B$, and an entity in $B$ is associated with any number (zero or more) of entities in $A$.
   - **E-R Notation:** Drawn with undirected lines connecting the relationship diamond to both entity sets.
   - **Example:** A student can enroll in multiple courses, and a course can enroll multiple students (`Takes`).

## 📖 High-Yield Exercises from the Book

### 🎯 HYE-01 · Practice Exercise 6.9 · Enforcing One-to-One Cardinality Constraints in Relational Tables

> **Source:** Silberschatz 7e · Chapter 6 (Practice Exercise 6.9)  
> **Exam Relevance:** High-Probability Relational Integrity Design Drill  
> **Key Tested Concept:** Translating one-to-one ($1:1$) E-R cardinality constraints into SQL relational schemas using primary keys, unique constraints, and foreign keys.

**Problem Statement:**
> Suppose the `advisor` relationship set between entity sets `student` and `instructor` were **one-to-one ($1:1$)** rather than many-to-one.
> 1. What extra relational integrity constraints are required on the relation `advisor` to ensure that the one-to-one cardinality constraint is strictly enforced?
> 2. Alternatively, if the `advisor` relationship is merged into the `student` relation, what constraint is necessary?
> 3. Provide both the relational schema notation and the SQL `CREATE TABLE` DDL.

**Model Exam-Style Solution:**

1. **Analysis of the $1:1$ Cardinality Rule:**
   - In standard university operations, `advisor` is many-to-one ($N:1$): multiple students can share one advisor, so primary key $\text{PK}(\text{advisor}) = \{s\_id\}$ suffices.
   - If `advisor` is **one-to-one ($1:1$)**:
     - Each student has at most one advisor.
     - Each instructor can advise at most one student.

2. **Case A: Separate Relationship Relation `advisor`:**
   - In a separate junction table `advisor(s_id, i_id)`:
     - Set $s\_id$ as the primary key:
       $$
       \text{Primary Key}(\text{advisor}) = \{s\_id\}
       $$
       This guarantees that each student appears at most once in the relationship.
     - **Extra Required Constraint:** Declare $i\_id$ as **`UNIQUE`**:
       $$
       \text{UNIQUE}(i\_id)
       $$
       This guarantees that no instructor can appear more than once, strictly enforcing the $1:1$ constraint from the instructor side.
     - Both attributes serve as foreign keys referencing `student(ID)` and `instructor(ID)` respectively.
   - *(Note: Alternatively, making $i\_id$ the primary key and declaring $s\_id$ as `UNIQUE` is mathematically equivalent.)*

   **SQL DDL Implementation:**
   ```sql
   CREATE TABLE advisor (
       s_id   VARCHAR(5),
       i_id   VARCHAR(5) NOT NULL,
       PRIMARY KEY (s_id),
       UNIQUE (i_id),
       FOREIGN KEY (s_id) REFERENCES student(ID) ON DELETE CASCADE,
       FOREIGN KEY (i_id) REFERENCES instructor(ID) ON DELETE CASCADE
   );
   ```

3. **Case B: Merged Table Schema (Combining `advisor` into `student`):**
   - Because every student has at most one advisor, $i\_id$ can be embedded directly into the `student` relation:
     $$
     \text{Student}(\underline{\text{ID}}, \text{name}, \text{dept\_name}, \text{tot\_cred}, \text{i\_id}^*)
     $$
   - **Extra Required Constraint:** To prevent two different student rows from holding the same instructor ID, `i_id` **must have a `UNIQUE` constraint**:
     ```sql
     ALTER TABLE student ADD CONSTRAINT unique_instructor_advisor UNIQUE (i_id);
     ```
   - If the participation of `student` is total, `i_id` must also be declared `NOT NULL`. $\blacksquare$

**💡 RUET Exam Takeaway:**
To enforce a $1:1$ relationship in relational tables, choosing a single primary key only guarantees $N:1$. You **must add a `UNIQUE` constraint** on the other entity's foreign key to prevent the "many" side from forming!

### 🎯 HYE-02 · Practice Exercise 6.10 · Enforcing Total Participation in Relational Tables Using NOT NULL Constraints

> **Source:** Silberschatz 7e · Chapter 6 (Practice Exercise 6.10)  
> **Exam Relevance:** High-Yield Relational Integrity Constraint Drill  
> **Key Tested Concept:** Guaranteeing total participation in SQL relational tables when combining $N:1$ relationships into entity tables.

**Problem Statement:**
> Consider a many-to-one ($N:1$) relationship set $R$ between entity sets $A$ (on the "many" side) and $B$ (on the "one" side).
> 1. Suppose the relation created from $R$ is combined with the relation created from $A$.
> 2. In SQL, attributes participating in a foreign key constraint default to permitting `NULL`. Explain how total participation of $A$ in $R$ can be strictly enforced using SQL constraints.
> 3. Contrast this with the case where participation of $A$ is partial.

**Model Exam-Style Solution:**

1. **Schema of Combined Relation:**
   - Because $R$ is many-to-one ($N:1$) from $A$ to $B$, each entity in $A$ is associated with at most one entity in $B$.
   - Rather than creating a separate table for $R$, standard E-R to relational reduction merges $R$ into the relation for $A$:
     $$
     \text{Schema}(A) = (\text{Attributes of } A) \cup (K_B) \cup (\text{Descriptive Attributes of } R)
     $$
     where $K_B$ is the primary key of $B$ stored as a foreign key inside table $A$.

2. **Enforcing Total Participation via `NOT NULL`:**
   - **Total Participation Condition:** Every entity $a \in A$ must participate in relationship $R$ with an existing entity in $B$.
   - **The SQL Null Vulnerability:** By default, SQL foreign keys permit `NULL` values. A tuple in $A$ with a `NULL` foreign key represents an entity that does not participate in $R$ (i.e., partial participation).
   - **Enforcement Mechanism:** To guarantee total participation, the foreign key column $K_B$ must be explicitly declared **`NOT NULL`**:
     ```sql
     CREATE TABLE A (
         a_id   VARCHAR(10) PRIMARY KEY,
         ...,
         b_id   VARCHAR(10) NOT NULL,
         FOREIGN KEY (b_id) REFERENCES B(b_id)
     );
     ```
   - **Why Both Constraints Are Essential:**
     - `NOT NULL` guarantees that no tuple in $A$ can exist without an associated $b\_id$.
     - `FOREIGN KEY` guarantees that the supplied $b\_id$ exists as a valid primary key in table $B$.
     - Together, they mathematically guarantee total participation of $A$ in $R$.

3. **Contrast with Partial Participation:**
   - If participation of $A$ in $R$ is **partial** (an entity in $A$ is allowed to exist without any relationship to $B$), the foreign key column $b\_id$ must omit the `NOT NULL` clause, allowing unassociated entities to store `NULL`. $\blacksquare$

**💡 RUET Exam Takeaway:**
In a merged $N:1$ table, **Total Participation $\iff$ `FOREIGN KEY` + `NOT NULL`**. If the prompt states *"every A must be associated with B"*, omitting `NOT NULL` causes a loss of marks!

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Arrow direction trap:** In Silberschatz Chen notation, a directed arrow points to the **ONE** side. Many students draw arrows toward the "Many" side. Remember: Arrow = "At most one", Line = "Many".
2. **Junction table primary key:** When converting an M:N relationship to a table, both foreign keys together form the composite primary key. Choosing only one foreign key is incorrect and causes duplicate key errors.
3. **Descriptive attributes:** Do not forget descriptive attributes on relationships (such as `grade` or `date`). They belong in the junction table, not in the entity tables.
4. **Participation double lines:** If a relationship has total participation, draw a double line between the entity set and the diamond.
