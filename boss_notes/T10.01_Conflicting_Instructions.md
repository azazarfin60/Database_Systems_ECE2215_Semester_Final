# T10.1: Conflicting Instructions and Conflict Serializability

> **Topic:** T10: Serializability | **Priority:** 🟢 Foundational Priority | **Exam Frequency:** 1/7 finals (2 marks: 2017 Q.5(b), plus part of 2021 Q.7(b))  
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 17 (§17.6, pp. 813–816) · Lecture Deck 07 (Slides 16–19) · Classnote by Raidah (T10, p. 26)

## Why This Topic Matters

Serial schedules run transactions one at a time and are naturally safe, whereas concurrent schedules interleave instructions to boost speed. How can the DBMS quickly tell if an interleaved schedule is safe? Instead of trying to understand the complex business math inside every transaction, the database looks only at `read` and `write` operations and checks whether any pair of steps **conflict**. Conflict serializability is the foundation for every precedence-graph problem you will solve in Unit 6.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Conflicting Instructions:** "We say that $I$ and $J$ **conflict** if they are operations by different transactions on the same data item, and at least one of these instructions is a $\text{write}$ operation."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §17.6 (p. 814)

> **Conflict Equivalent:** "If a schedule $S$ can be transformed into a schedule $S'$ by a series of swaps of nonconflicting instructions, we say that $S$ and $S'$ are **conflict equivalent**."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §17.6 (p. 815)

> **Conflict Serializable:** "We say that a schedule $S$ is **conflict serializable** if it is conflict equivalent to a serial schedule."  
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §17.6 (p. 815)

## 1. When Do Two Instructions Conflict?

### Intuitive Analogy: Looking at a Whiteboard vs. Erasing It

Imagine two students, $T_1$ and $T_2$, working with a number written on a classroom whiteboard ($Q$):
- If $T_1$ looks at the board and then $T_2$ looks at the board ($\text{read}$ / $\text{read}$), does it matter who looked first? Not at all! Both see the exact same number, and the board does not change.
- Now suppose $T_1$ looks at the board and $T_2$ erases it to write a new number ($\text{read}$ / $\text{write}$). Here, the order changes everything! If $T_1$ looks first, $T_1$ sees the old number. If $T_2$ writes first, $T_1$ sees the new number.
- Similarly, if both students erase the board and write their own numbers ($\text{write}$ / $\text{write}$), whoever writes *second* leaves the final number on the board.

That is the entire secret of conflicting instructions: **order only matters when two different transactions touch the exact same data item and at least one of them writes to it.**

### The Three Requirements for a Conflict

Two instructions $I_i$ and $I_j$ conflict if and only if they meet all three conditions at once:
1. **Different Transactions:** $I_i$ belongs to transaction $T_i$ and $I_j$ belongs to a different transaction $T_j$ ($i \neq j$).
2. **Same Data Item:** Both instructions access the exact same database item $Q$.
3. **At Least One Write:** At least one of the two instructions is a $\text{write}(Q)$ operation.

Here are the four possible combinations of $\text{read}$ and $\text{write}$ on the same item $Q$:

| Case | Instruction $I_i$ ($T_i$) | Instruction $I_j$ ($T_j$) | Conflict? | Why Order Matters (or Doesn't) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **1** | $\text{read}(Q)$ | $\text{read}(Q)$ | **No** | Neither transaction changes $Q$. Swapping their order does not change what either transaction reads. |
| **2** | $\text{read}(Q)$ | $\text{write}(Q)$ | **Yes (RW)** | If $T_i$ reads first, it sees the old value of $Q$. If $T_j$ writes first, $T_i$ sees the new value written by $T_j$. |
| **3** | $\text{write}(Q)$ | $\text{read}(Q)$ | **Yes (WR)** | If $T_i$ writes first, $T_j$ reads the updated value. If $T_j$ reads first, $T_j$ gets the old value. |
| **4** | $\text{write}(Q)$ | $\text{write}(Q)$ | **Yes (WW)** | Both overwrite $Q$. Whichever transaction writes *second* determines the value left in the database. |

### Two Golden Rules to Remember

1. **Different Data Items Never Conflict:** If $I_i$ touches item $A$ and $I_j$ touches item $B$ ($A \neq B$), they **never** conflict, even if both are writes. Updating account $A$ and updating account $B$ are completely independent actions, so you can swap their order without changing anything.
2. **Never Swap Inside the Same Transaction:** Instructions that belong to the **same** transaction ($T_i = T_j$) are locked in their program order. A transaction obviously cannot write its result before reading its input!

## 2. Conflict Equivalence and Conflict Serializability

When two adjacent instructions from different transactions do **not** conflict, swapping their order leaves the outcome of the schedule 100% unchanged: every transaction still reads the exact same inputs, and the database still ends up with the exact same final values.

- **Conflict Equivalence:** Two schedules $S$ and $S'$ are **conflict equivalent** if you can turn $S$ into $S'$ by repeatedly swapping adjacent non-conflicting instructions.
- **Conflict Serializability:** A concurrent schedule $S$ is **conflict serializable** if it is conflict equivalent to some serial schedule.

### Illustrative Mini-Example: Spotting Conflicts in a 4-Step Schedule

Consider this short schedule $S$ over transactions $T_1$ and $T_2$:

$$
S:\quad T_1:\text{read}(A),\quad T_2:\text{read}(A),\quad T_1:\text{write}(B),\quad T_2:\text{write}(A)
$$

- Look at Step 2 ($T_2:\text{read}(A)$) and Step 3 ($T_1:\text{write}(B)$). Do they conflict? **No**, because one touches $A$ and the other touches $B$ ($A \neq B$).
- If we swap Step 2 and Step 3, we get:

$$
S':\quad T_1:\text{read}(A),\quad T_1:\text{write}(B),\quad T_2:\text{read}(A),\quad T_2:\text{write}(A)
$$

- Look at $S'$ now: all of $T_1$'s steps run first, followed by all of $T_2$'s steps! Because one valid swap turned $S$ into the serial schedule $\langle T_1, T_2 \rangle$, schedule $S$ is **conflict serializable**.
- Notice why we could *not* swap $T_1:\text{read}(A)$ past $T_2:\text{write}(A)$: both touch $A$ and one is a write (an RW conflict), so $T_1$ must stay ahead of $T_2$.

## 3. Testing Conflict Serializability: The Precedence Graph

Swapping instructions by hand gets messy when a schedule has 15 or 20 steps. Instead, we use a **precedence graph** (also called a **serialization graph**) to spot conflicts visually.

### Intuitive Meaning of a Precedence Graph Edge

Every conflicting pair of instructions acts like a one-way street sign between two transactions. If $T_1$ reads $A$ before $T_2$ writes $A$, we cannot swap those two steps, so $T_1$ **must** come before $T_2$ in any equivalent serial schedule. We record that rule by drawing an arrow $T_1 \longrightarrow T_2$.

### Graph Construction Rules

For a schedule $S$, we build a directed graph $G = (V, E)$:
- **Vertices ($V$):** Draw one node (circle) for each transaction $T_i$ in the schedule.
- **Directed Edges ($E$):** Draw a directed arrow $T_i \longrightarrow T_j$ ($i \neq j$) whenever an operation in $T_i$ runs **before** a conflicting operation in $T_j$ on the same item $Q$:
  1. **WR conflict:** $T_i$ executes $\text{write}(Q)$ before $T_j$ executes $\text{read}(Q)$.
  2. **RW conflict:** $T_i$ executes $\text{read}(Q)$ before $T_j$ executes $\text{write}(Q)$.
  3. **WW conflict:** $T_i$ executes $\text{write}(Q)$ before $T_j$ executes $\text{write}(Q)$.

### The Conflict Serializability Theorem

> **Conflict Serializability Theorem:**  
> A schedule $S$ is conflict serializable if and only if its precedence graph $G$ has **no directed cycles** (meaning $G$ is a Directed Acyclic Graph, or DAG).

- **If there is a cycle** (like $T_1 \to T_2 \to T_1$), $T_1$ would have to run both before and after $T_2$, which is impossible in a serial schedule! Thus, a cycle proves the schedule is **not** conflict serializable.
- **If there are no cycles**, we can read off the equivalent serial order using **topological sorting** (starting with transactions that have no incoming arrows).

---

## 4. Conflict Serializability vs. View Serializability

Conflict serializability is slightly stricter than **view serializability**:

$$
\text{Conflict Serializable Schedules} \subset \text{View Serializable Schedules}
$$

1. Every conflict serializable schedule is automatically view serializable (and therefore preserves consistency).
2. A schedule can be view serializable *without* being conflict serializable only if it contains **blind writes** (where a transaction writes a value without reading it first).
3. Real database engines use conflict serializability because checking a precedence graph for cycles takes fast polynomial time $O(V + E)$, whereas testing view serializability is an NP-complete problem.

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2017 Q.5(b) · Conflict Serializability Definition and Conditions

> **Appeared:** 2017 Q.5(b) (2 marks)

> **Question:**
>
> What do you know about conflict serializability?

**Full Answer:**

**Conflict serializability** is the primary standard used by database systems to verify whether a concurrent schedule is safe. A schedule $S$ is **conflict serializable** if it is **conflict equivalent** to a serial schedule, meaning that $S$ can be transformed into a serial schedule by a series of swaps of adjacent non-conflicting instructions.

#### 1. Rules for Conflicting Instructions
Two instructions $I_i$ and $I_j$ from different transactions ($T_i \neq T_j$) **conflict** if and only if they access the **same data item $Q$** and **at least one of them is a $\text{write}$ operation**:
1. $I_i = \text{read}(Q)$ and $I_j = \text{read}(Q)$: **Do not conflict** (two reads do not modify data, so their order does not matter).
2. $I_i = \text{read}(Q)$ and $I_j = \text{write}(Q)$: **Conflict** (swapping them changes whether $T_i$ reads the old or new value).
3. $I_i = \text{write}(Q)$ and $I_j = \text{read}(Q)$: **Conflict** (swapping them changes the value read by $T_j$).
4. $I_i = \text{write}(Q)$ and $I_j = \text{write}(Q)$: **Conflict** (swapping them changes which write stays in the database last).

*(Note: Operations on different data items, such as $A \neq B$, never conflict).*

#### 2. Testing via Precedence Graph
A schedule $S$ is conflict serializable if and only if its **precedence graph** (where nodes are transactions and directed edges $T_i \to T_j$ represent conflicts where $T_i$ acts before $T_j$) contains **no directed cycles**. If the graph is acyclic, a topological sort yields the equivalent serial order.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Two Reads Never Conflict:** Writing that $\text{read}(Q)$ and $\text{read}(Q)$ conflict is the single most common error on this question. Remember the whiteboard rule: two people looking at a number never change it.
2. **Check the Variable Name First:** Before checking read vs. write, check whether the two steps touch the same variable. $\text{write}(A)$ and $\text{write}(B)$ do **not** conflict because $A \neq B$.
3. **Same Transaction = No Conflict Edge:** You only check conflicts between *different* transactions ($T_i \neq T_j$). Never draw a self-loop $T_1 \to T_1$ in a precedence graph.
4. **Checklist for 2 Marks:** Define conflict equivalence + conflict serializability, list the 4 $\text{read}/\text{write}$ cases on item $Q$, and state that the precedence graph must be acyclic.
