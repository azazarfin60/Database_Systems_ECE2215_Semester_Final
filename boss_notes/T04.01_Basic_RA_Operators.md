# T04.1: Basic Relational Algebra Operators

> **Topic:** T04 — Relational Algebra & Calculus | **Priority:** ⭐ | **Exam Frequency:** 0/7 finals (Foundation for 48 marks in T04.2–T04.3)
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 2 (§2.6, pp. 48–56) · Lecture Deck 02 (Slides 9–26) · Raidah Class Notes (T04, pp. 13R–14L)

## Why This Topic Matters

Relational algebra is the mathematical language behind SQL. Every operation in relational algebra takes one or two tables as input, applies a clean rule, and produces a brand-new table as output. Because the output is always another table, you can chain operations together like building blocks to answer complex questions. Even though exams rarely ask pure definitions of these operators on their own, you will use them constantly in almost every semester final to write queries (T04.2) and explain expressions in plain English (T04.3), which together account for 48 marks.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Relational Algebra:** "The relational algebra is a functional query language... The relational algebra forms the theoretical basis of the SQL query language." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.5–2.6 (p. 48)

> **Select Operation:** "The select operation selects tuples that satisfy a given predicate. We use the lowercase Greek letter sigma ($\sigma$) to denote selection. The predicate appears as a subscript to $\sigma$." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.1 (p. 49)

> **Project Operation:** "The project operation is a unary operation that returns its argument relation, with certain attributes left out. Since a relation is a set, any duplicate rows are eliminated." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.2 (p. 49)

> **Union Compatibility:** "1. We must ensure that the input relations to the union operation have the same number of attributes; the number of attributes of a relation is referred to as its arity. 2. When the attributes have associated types, the types of the $i$th attributes of both input relations must be the same, for each $i$. Such relations are referred to as compatible relations." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.6 (p. 54)

> **Rename Operation:** "The results of relational-algebra expressions do not have a name that we can use to refer to them. It is useful in some cases to give them names; the rename operator, denoted by the lowercase Greek letter rho ($\rho$), lets us do this." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.8 (p. 56)

## Overview of Fundamental and Derived Operators

### Intuitive Analogy: Why We Have Two Groups of Operators

Think of the **six fundamental operators** like the basic arithmetic buttons ($+, -, \times$) on a calculator. They are the minimum set of operations needed to express any basic query, and none of them can be built out of the others. The **derived operators** are like shortcut buttons: they do not add new mathematical power, but they combine several basic steps into one compact symbol so your queries stay short and readable.

1. **Six Fundamental Operators:**
   - Select ($\sigma$) for horizontal row filtering
   - Project ($\Pi$) for vertical column slicing
   - Cartesian Product ($\times$) for pairing rows across two tables
   - Union ($\cup$) for merging two compatible sets of rows
   - Set Difference ($-$) for removing rows of one table from another
   - Rename ($\rho$) for giving a table or its columns a new alias

2. **Derived (Additional) Operators:**
   - Set Intersection ($\cap$)
   - Natural Join ($\bowtie$)
   - Theta Join ($\bowtie_\theta$)
   - Assignment ($\leftarrow$)

Every derived operator can be rewritten using only the six fundamental operators.

## The Six Fundamental Operators

### 1. Select Operation ($\sigma$)

**Intuition:** Think of $\sigma$ as a **horizontal filter** (just like the `WHERE` clause in SQL). It scans a table row by row and keeps only the rows that pass a condition, without changing any columns.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  \sigma_{p}(r)
  $$

  Here, $p$ is the filtering condition (selection predicate) and $r$ is the input relation.

- **Operators Allowed in $p$:**
  - Comparison operators: $=, \neq, <, \le, >, \ge$
  - Logical connectives: $\land$ (AND), $\lor$ (OR), $\neg$ (NOT)
  - Comparisons between two attributes or between an attribute and a constant

- **Example:**
  Find all instructors in the Physics department who earn more than \$90,000:

  $$
  \sigma_{\text{dept\_name} = \text{"Physics"} \land \text{salary} > 90000}(\text{instructor})
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* We scan the `instructor` table and keep only those rows where `dept_name` equals `"Physics"` and `salary` is strictly greater than `90000`. All columns of `instructor` remain in the output.

### 2. Project Operation ($\Pi$)

**Intuition:** Think of $\Pi$ as a **vertical slicer** (like `SELECT DISTINCT col1, col2` in SQL). Whereas $\sigma$ picks which *rows* to keep, $\Pi$ picks which *columns* to keep and throws away the rest.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  \Pi_{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_k}(r)
  $$

  Here, $A_1, \dots, A_k$ are the columns you want in the final table.

- **Important Rule (Automatic Duplicate Elimination):**
  Because relational algebra treats every relation as a true mathematical set (not a bag with duplicates like SQL), if dropping columns makes two rows look identical, $\Pi$ automatically removes the duplicates so every output row is unique.

- **Example:**
  List all distinct department names from the `instructor` table:

  $$
  \Pi_{\text{dept\_name}}(\text{instructor})
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* We take the `instructor` relation, discard `ID`, `name`, and `salary`, and keep only the `dept_name` column with all duplicate department names removed.

### 3. Cartesian Product Operation ($\times$)

**Intuition:** On its own, $\times$ is a brute-force combiner that pairs **every row** of the first table with **every row** of the second table. Most of those pairings are meaningless until you follow $\times$ with a selection ($\sigma$) that keeps only matching keys.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  r \times s
  $$

- **Mathematical Properties:**
  - **Columns (Arity):** If $r$ has $n_1$ columns and $s$ has $n_2$ columns, the result has $n_1 + n_2$ columns.
  - **Rows (Cardinality):** If $r$ has $m_1$ rows and $s$ has $m_2$ rows, the result has $m_1 \times m_2$ rows.
  - **Naming Conflicts:** If both tables have a column with the same name $A$, we prefix them with the table names ($r.A$ and $s.A$) so we can tell them apart.

- **Example:**
  Pair every instructor row with every teaching assignment row:

  $$
  \text{instructor} \times \text{teaches}
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* Every tuple in `instructor` is glued side-by-side with every tuple in `teaches`, producing a wide table with columns `(instructor.ID, name, dept_name, salary, teaches.ID, course_id, sec_id, semester, year)`.

### 4. Union Operation ($\cup$)

**Intuition:** Union stacks two tables vertically into a single list and automatically removes any duplicate rows that appear in both tables.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  r \cup s
  $$

- **Why Union Compatibility Works:**
  You can only stack two tables vertically if their columns line up cleanly. That is why $r$ and $s$ must be **union-compatible**:
  1. Both tables must have the **same arity** (the exact same number of columns).
  2. Each column position from left to right must have a **compatible domain** (for example, you cannot stack a column of student names on top of a column of salaries).

- **Example:**
  Find the IDs of all courses taught in Fall 2017, Spring 2018, or both:

  $$
  \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Fall"} \land \text{year} = 2017}(\text{section})) \cup \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Spring"} \land \text{year} = 2018}(\text{section}))
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* First, on the left side, we filter `section` for Fall 2017 rows and project just `course_id`. Next, on the right side, we filter `section` for Spring 2018 rows and project `course_id`. Because both sides now have a single `course_id` column, we combine them with $\cup$ to get all unique course IDs from either semester.

### 5. Set Difference Operation ($-$)

**Intuition:** Set difference ($r - s$) starts with all rows in $r$ and crosses out any row that also appears in $s$. In exams, this is your go-to operator whenever a question says **"only"**, **"but not"**, or **"never"**.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  r - s
  $$

- **Requirements:**
  Just like union, $r$ and $s$ must be union-compatible before you can subtract one from the other.

- **Example:**
  Find all courses taught in Fall 2017 **but not** in Spring 2018:

  $$
  \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Fall"} \land \text{year} = 2017}(\text{section})) - \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Spring"} \land \text{year} = 2018}(\text{section}))
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we project `course_id` for all Fall 2017 sections. Next we project `course_id` for all Spring 2018 sections. Finally, the $-$ operator removes any Fall 2017 course ID that also shows up in the Spring 2018 list.

### 6. Rename Operation ($\rho$)

**Intuition:** Whenever you need to compare rows within the **same table** (for example, finding instructors who earn more than another instructor), joining `instructor` with `instructor` creates a name clash because both copies have the exact same table and column names. The rename operator $\rho$ gives a table (and optionally its columns) a fresh alias, just like `FROM instructor AS d` in SQL.

- **Syntax Form 1 (Rename the Table Only):**

  $$
  \rho_{x}(E)
  $$

  Takes the result of expression $E$ and names it $x$.

- **Syntax Form 2 (Rename Both the Table and Its Columns):**

  $$
  \rho_{x(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n)}(E)
  $$

  Takes the result of $E$, names the table $x$, and renames its columns to $A_1, \dots, A_n$.

- **Example:**
  Find the names of all instructors who earn more than instructor `12121` (Wu):

  $$
  \Pi_{\text{instructor.name}}(\sigma_{\text{instructor.salary} > d.\text{salary}}(\text{instructor} \times \rho_{d}(\sigma_{\text{ID} = \text{"12121"}}(\text{instructor}))))
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we filter `instructor` for `ID = "12121"` and rename that 1-row result to $d$ using $\rho_d$. Next we take the Cartesian product of the full `instructor` table with $d$, filter for rows where `instructor.salary > d.salary`, and finally project `instructor.name`.

## Derived Operators and Extensions

### 1. Set Intersection ($\cap$)

**Intuition:** Intersection keeps only the rows that appear in **both** $r$ and $s$. Why is it a derived operator? Because keeping what is common to $r$ and $s$ is the exact same thing as starting with $r$ and subtracting everything in $r$ that is missing from $s$:

- **Definition:**

  $$
  r \cap s = r - (r - s)
  $$

- **Example:**
  Find courses taught in **both** Fall 2017 and Spring 2018:

  $$
  \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Fall"} \land \text{year} = 2017}(\text{section})) \cap \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Spring"} \land \text{year} = 2018}(\text{section}))
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we project `course_id` from Fall 2017 sections, then we project `course_id` from Spring 2018 sections, and finally $\cap$ keeps only the course IDs that appear in both sets.

### 2. Theta Join ($\bowtie_\theta$)

**Intuition:** Writing $\sigma_\theta(r \times s)$ over and over gets cluttered. Theta join ($\bowtie_\theta$) packages the Cartesian product and the filtering condition $\theta$ into one clean symbol:

- **Definition:**

  $$
  r \bowtie_\theta s = \sigma_\theta(r \times s)
  $$

  Here $\theta$ is any comparison condition linking columns of $r$ and $s$.

### 3. Natural Join ($\bowtie$)

**Intuition:** Most of the time when we join two tables, we want to match rows where their shared foreign-key/primary-key column names have equal values. Natural join ($\bowtie$) automates this completely: it finds every column name shared by both tables, checks that their values match, and drops the duplicate copy of the shared column(s).

- **Definition:**
  If $R$ and $S$ share attributes $R \cap S = \{A_1, \dots, A_k\}$, then:

  $$
  r \bowtie s = \Pi_{R \cup S}(\sigma_{r.A_1 = s.A_1 \land \dots \land r.A_k = s.A_k}(r \times s))
  $$

- **Example:**
  Match instructors with the course sections they teach:

  $$
  \text{instructor} \bowtie \text{teaches}
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* Both `instructor` and `teaches` have a column named `ID` (and no other common column name). The natural join pairs rows where `instructor.ID = teaches.ID` and keeps `ID` only once in the output.

### 4. Assignment Operation ($\leftarrow$)

**Intuition:** Just like storing an intermediate calculation in a variable in programming, the assignment operator ($\leftarrow$) lets you save the output of a sub-query into a temporary table name.

- **Syntax:**

  $$
  \text{Temp} \leftarrow E
  $$

- **Why You Should Use It in Exams:**
  Instead of cramming five nested operators into one long line with ten parentheses, break your query into 2 or 3 named steps:

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{FallCourses} &\leftarrow \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Fall"} \land \text{year} = 2017}(\text{section})) \\
  \text{SpringCourses} &\leftarrow \Pi_{\text{course\_id}}(\sigma_{\text{semester} = \text{"Spring"} \land \text{year} = 2018}(\text{section})) \\
  \text{Result} &\leftarrow \text{FallCourses} - \text{SpringCourses}
  \end{aligned}
  $$

  *Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we store Fall 2017 course IDs in `FallCourses`. Next we store Spring 2018 course IDs in `SpringCourses`. Finally we subtract `SpringCourses` from `FallCourses` and store the answer in `Result`.

### 5. Relational Algebra Database Modifications (Silberschatz 6e Notation)

**Intuition:** Because relational algebra expressions only compute new sets of rows without modifying tables in place, we express insertions, deletions, and updates by computing the new set of rows and overwriting the original table variable using $\leftarrow$:

- **Deletion:** Subtract the unwanted rows $E$ from relation $r$:

  $$
  r \leftarrow r - E
  $$

- **Insertion:** Union the new rows $E$ into relation $r$:

  $$
  r \leftarrow r \cup E
  $$

- **Update:** If you are updating every row in $r$, project the recalculated expressions directly:

  $$
  r \leftarrow \Pi_{F_1, F_2, \dots, F_n}(r)
  $$

  If you are updating only *some* rows in $r$, subtract the old versions of those rows and union in their newly updated versions:

  $$
  r \leftarrow (r - \text{OldTuples}) \cup \text{NewTuples}
  $$

## 🔮 Likely Questions

### 🎯 LQ1 · Six Fundamental Operators and Union Compatibility

**Question:**
List the six basic operators of relational algebra. State the conditions required for two relations to be union-compatible, and explain why this condition is necessary.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Six Basic Operators of Relational Algebra
1. **Select ($\sigma$):** Unary operator that filters tuples satisfying a given predicate.
2. **Project ($\Pi$):** Unary operator that outputs specified attributes and removes duplicates.
3. **Cartesian Product ($\times$):** Binary operator that pairs every tuple of relation $r$ with every tuple of relation $s$.
4. **Union ($\cup$):** Binary operator that combines tuples from two relations into a single set.
5. **Set Difference ($- $):** Binary operator that outputs tuples present in the first relation but absent from the second.
6. **Rename ($\rho$):** Unary operator that renames relation names or attribute names.

#### 2. Union Compatibility Conditions
For two relations $r$ and $s$ to undergo set operations ($\cup, \cap, -$):
1. **Equal Arity:** Both relations must have the exact same number of attributes.
2. **Compatible Attribute Domains:** For every column position $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, the domain of the $i$-th attribute of $r$ must match the domain of the $i$-th attribute of $s$.

#### 3. Why It Is Necessary
Relational algebra models data mathematically as relations over Cartesian products of domains ($r \subseteq D_1 \times D_2 \times \dots \times D_n$). Without equal arity and matching column domains, combining tuples creates ill-defined tuples where values have no common semantic domain (for example, attempting to combine student names with monetary budget numbers).

### 🎯 LQ2 · Distinguish Cartesian Product, Theta Join, and Natural Join

**Question:**
Differentiate among Cartesian product ($\times$), theta join ($\bowtie_\theta$), and natural join ($\bowtie$) with clear mathematical definitions and examples.

**Full Answer:**

| Feature | Cartesian Product ($r \times s$) | Theta Join ($r \bowtie_\theta s$) | Natural Join ($r \bowtie s$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Pairs every tuple of $r$ with every tuple of $s$. | Cartesian product followed by selection on condition $\theta$. | Matches tuples having equal values on all shared attribute names. |
| **Formula** | $r \times s$ | $\sigma_\theta(r \times s)$ | $\Pi_{R \cup S}(\sigma_{\bigwedge (r.A_i = s.A_i)}(r \times s))$ |
| **Attribute Handling** | Retains all attributes from both schemas, prefixing duplicates ($r.A, s.A$). | Retains all attributes from both schemas, prefixing duplicates. | Eliminates duplicate shared attributes, displaying each shared column once. |
| **Join Condition** | No condition required. | Explicit condition $\theta$ provided by user. | Implicit equality on all identically named attributes. |
| **Result Size** | Always $m_1 \times m_2$ tuples. | At most $m_1 \times m_2$ tuples. | At most $m_1 \times m_2$ tuples. |

**Concrete Example:**
Consider $r(A, B) = \{(1, 2)\}$ and $s(B, C) = \{(2, 3), (4, 5)\}$.
- **Cartesian Product:**
  $$
  r \times s = \{(1, 2, 2, 3), (1, 2, 4, 5)\} \quad \text{over schema } (r.A, r.B, s.B, s.C)
  $$
- **Theta Join ($r \bowtie_{r.B < s.B} s$):**
  $$
  r \bowtie_{r.B < s.B} s = \{(1, 2, 4, 5)\}
  $$
- **Natural Join ($r \bowtie s$):**
  Matches on shared attribute $B$ where $r.B = s.B = 2$:
  $$
  r \bowtie s = \{(1, 2, 3)\} \quad \text{over schema } (A, B, C)
  $$

### 🎯 LQ3 · Relational Algebra Update and Salary Increase

**Question:**
Explain how relation modifications (insertions, deletions, and updates) are expressed in relational algebra. Demonstrate giving a 10% salary raise to all employees in the "Hardware" department using relation assignment.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. General Relation Modification Syntax
In relational algebra, modifications are modeled by computing target tuples and updating relation variables:
- **Insertion:** $r \leftarrow r \cup E$
- **Deletion:** $r \leftarrow r - E$
- **Update:** $r \leftarrow (r - \text{OldTuples}) \cup \text{NewTuples}$

#### 2. Salary Raise Walkthrough
Consider relation $\text{works}(\text{person\_name}, \text{company\_name}, \text{salary})$.
Suppose we want to give a 10% raise to all employees who work for "Hardware":

1. **Identify the affected employees:**
   $$
   T_{\text{affected}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{company\_name} = \text{"Hardware"}}(\text{works})
   $$

2. **Compute updated tuples with 10% raise:**
   $$
   T_{\text{updated}} \leftarrow \Pi_{\text{person\_name}, \text{company\_name}, \text{salary} * 1.1}(T_{\text{affected}})
   $$

3. **Replace old tuples with new tuples in `works`:**
   $$
   \text{works} \leftarrow (\text{works} - T_{\text{affected}}) \cup T_{\text{updated}}
   $$

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Set Semantics vs. SQL Multiset:** In relational algebra, $\Pi_{A}(r)$ automatically deletes duplicate rows. In SQL, `SELECT A FROM r` preserves duplicates unless `DISTINCT` is specified.
2. **Natural Join Shared Column Trap:** Natural join matches ALL columns with identical names. If two relations have both `ID` and `name`, $r \bowtie s$ requires both $r.\text{ID} = s.\text{ID}$ and $r.\text{name} = s.\text{name}$. If you only want to match on `ID`, use theta join: $r \bowtie_{r.\text{ID} = s.\text{ID}} s$.
3. **Use Assignment ($\leftarrow$) in Long Queries:** Writing a gigantic one-line nested expression often leads to parenthesis errors. Break your answer into named intermediate relations using $\leftarrow$. RUET examiners award full marks for clean, stepwise assignments.
