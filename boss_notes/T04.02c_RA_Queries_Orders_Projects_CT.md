# T04.2c: RA Queries — Customer Orders, Projects, and CT Drills

> **Topic:** T04 — Relational Algebra & Calculus | **Priority:** 🔴 | **Exam Frequency:** 6/7 finals (+ CT-01 Q4) (41 marks in T04.2)
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 2 (§2.6), Ch. 16 (§16.1) · Lecture Deck 02 (Slides 8–29) · Raidah Class Notes (pp. 13–14)

## Why This Topic Matters

In the 2021 and 2024 finals (as well as Class Test 01), RUET examiners tested relational algebra queries on three practical schemas: an e-commerce **Customer-Order** database, a company **Employee-Project (`WORKS_ON`)** database, and a university **Student-Course-Alumni** database. These questions focus on three core skills: filtering with numeric comparisons ($\text{price} < 300$, $\text{Marks} \ge 80$), finding people associated with **more than one** record (using either $\gamma$ grouping or a self-join), and projecting tables down to matching columns before taking a union ($\cup$).

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Rename Operation:** "Unlike relations in the database, the results of relational-algebra expressions do not have a name that we can use to refer to them. It is useful in some cases to give them names; the **rename** operator, denoted by the lowercase Greek letter rho ($\rho$), lets us do this." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.8 (p. 56)

> **Union Compatibility:** "In general, for a union operation to make sense: 1. We must ensure that the input relations to the union operation have the same number of attributes; the number of attributes of a relation is referred to as its **arity**. 2. When the attributes have associated types, the types of the $i$th attributes of both input relations must be the same, for each $i$. Such relations are referred to as **compatible** relations." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.6 (pp. 54–55)

> **Cartesian-Product Operation:** "The **Cartesian-product** operation, denoted by a cross ($\times$), allows us to combine information from any two relations." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §2.6.4 (p. 51)

> **Aggregate Functions:** "Aggregate functions are functions that take a collection (a set or multiset) of values as input and return a single value." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §3.7 (p. 83)

## Relational Query Formulation on Enterprise Schemas

In retail, project-management, and university schemas, a main entity table (`customer`, `EMPLOYEE`, or `STUDENT`) connects to transaction or assignment tables through a shared ID column:

```text
Enterprise Schema Architectural Relationships
┌──────────────────┐               ┌──────────────────┐
│     customer     │ 1           * │      order       │
│ (id, name, age)  ├───────────────┤ (id, product,    │
│                  │               │  price, date)    │
└──────────────────┘               └──────────────────┘

┌──────────────────┐               ┌──────────────────┐               ┌──────────────────┐
│     EMPLOYEE     │ 1           * │     WORKS_ON     │ *           1 │     PROJECT      │
│ (EID, Name,      ├───────────────┤    (EID, PID,    ├───────────────┤    (PID, Name,   │
│  Dept, Salary)   │               │     Hours)       │               │     Budget)      │
└──────────────────┘               └──────────────────┘               └──────────────────┘
```

### 1. How to Find Entities with "Multiple" Records (At Least Two)
Suppose a question asks you to find customers who ordered **more than one distinct product** in `order(id, product, price, date)`, or employees working on **multiple projects** (tested in 2024 Q.2(b)). There are two ways to solve this in relational algebra:

#### Method 1: Grouping and Counting with $\gamma$ (Fastest in Exams)
**Intuition:** Just like `GROUP BY id HAVING COUNT(product) > 1` in SQL, we group the transaction table by the person's ID, count how many items they have, and filter for counts strictly greater than 1.

For example, to find customer IDs who ordered more than one product:

$$
T_{\text{counts}} \leftarrow {}_{\text{id}}\gamma_{\text{count-distinct}(\text{product}) \ \text{as} \ \text{prod\_count}}(\text{order})
$$

$$
\Pi_{\text{id}}(\sigma_{\text{prod\_count} > 1}(T_{\text{counts}}))
$$

*Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we group `order` by `id` and count distinct products per customer. Then we filter for rows where `prod_count > 1` and project `id`.

#### Method 2: Pure Relational Algebra via Self-Join (No $\gamma$ Needed)
**Intuition:** What if you want to use only the six basic operators without $\gamma$? If a customer bought at least two different products, then if we make two copies of the `order` table ($O_1$ and $O_2$) and pair them up, there must be a pair of rows with the **same customer ID** ($O_1.\text{id} = O_2.\text{id}$) but **different products** ($O_1.\text{product} \neq O_2.\text{product}$):

$$
O_1 \leftarrow \rho_{O_1}(\text{order}), \qquad O_2 \leftarrow \rho_{O_2}(\text{order})
$$

$$
\Pi_{O_1.\text{id}}(\sigma_{O_1.\text{id} = O_2.\text{id} \land O_1.\text{product} \neq O_2.\text{product}}(O_1 \times O_2))
$$

*Step-by-step walkthrough:* First we rename two copies of `order` as $O_1$ and $O_2$. Next we take their Cartesian product and filter for pairs that share the same `id` while having different `product` values. Finally we project $O_1.\text{id}$. Writing both methods on an exam script guarantees full marks under any grading rubric!

## Union Compatibility and Pre-Projection Rules

**Intuition:** A classic exam trap (tested in CT-01 Q.04) asks you to combine two tables that have different numbers of columns, such as:
- $\text{STUDENT}(\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}, \text{Dept\_Name}, \text{CGPA})$ — **4 columns (arity 4)**
- $\text{ALUMNI}(\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}, \text{Dept\_Name})$ — **3 columns (arity 3)**

If you write $\text{STUDENT} \cup \text{ALUMNI}$ directly, the expression is invalid because you cannot stack a 4-column table on top of a 3-column table. Instead, you must **project both tables down to the exact same target columns first**, and then take their union:

```text
Direct Union (FAILS):
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ STUDENT: (Student_ID, Name, Dept_Name, CGPA) │  Arity = 4
└──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                       │ 
                       ✕ Cannot Union (Arity 4 ≠ Arity 3)
                       │
┌──────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
│ ALUMNI:  (Student_ID, Name, Dept_Name)       │  Arity = 3
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Pre-Projected Union (SUCCEEDS):
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Π_{Student_ID, Name}(STUDENT)                │  Arity = 2
└──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                       │
                       ▼ ∪ Union Operator Valid
                       │
┌──────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
│ Π_{Student_ID, Name}(ALUMNI)                 │  Arity = 2
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Worked Example: High-Value Customer Orders

Consider the customer-order schema:
- $\text{customer}(\underline{\text{id}}, \text{name}, \text{age}, \text{gender})$
- $\text{order}(\underline{\text{id}}, \text{product}, \text{price}, \text{date})$

### Problem Statement
Find the names of all female customers who placed an order for a `"laptop"` costing at least \$1,000 during the year 2023.

### Step-by-Step Derivation

1. **First, filter the orders:** We filter `order` using $\sigma$ for rows where `product = "laptop"`, `price >= 1000`, and `year(date) = 2023`:

$$
T_{\text{qualifying\_orders}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{product} = \text{"laptop"} \land \text{price} \ge 1000 \land \text{year}(\text{date}) = 2023}(\text{order})
$$

2. **Next, filter the customers:** We filter `customer` using $\sigma$ for rows where `gender = "female"`:

$$
T_{\text{female\_customers}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{gender} = \text{"female"}}(\text{customer})
$$

3. **Finally, join and project names:** We natural-join $T_{\text{female\_customers}}$ and $T_{\text{qualifying\_orders}}$ on their shared column `id`, and project `name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{name}}(T_{\text{female\_customers}} \bowtie T_{\text{qualifying\_orders}})
$$

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2021 Q.2(a) · Customer and Order Database Queries

> **Appeared:** 2021 Q.2(a) — (5 marks)

**Question:**

Consider the following relation schema:  
  customer (<u>id</u>, name, age, gender)  
  order (<u>id</u>, product, price, date)

Now, give expression in relational algebra to-  
(i) find name of the customers who are male.  
(ii) find name of all customers who have purchased car as product.  
(iii) find the details of all customers who have purchased "phone" as product and the price is less than \$300.

**Full Answer:**

*Assumption: The attribute `order.id` is a foreign key referencing `customer.id`.*

#### (i) Names of male customers

First we filter `customer` using $\sigma$ for rows where `gender = "male"`, and then we project `name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{name}}(\sigma_{\text{gender} = \text{"male"}}(\text{customer}))
$$

#### (ii) Names of all customers who purchased car as product

1. First we filter `order` using $\sigma$ for rows where `product = "car"`:

$$
T_{\text{car\_orders}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{product} = \text{"car"}}(\text{order})
$$

2. Next we natural-join `customer` with $T_{\text{car\_orders}}$ on `id` and project `name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{name}}(\text{customer} \bowtie T_{\text{car\_orders}})
$$

Or as a single nested expression:

$$
\Pi_{\text{name}}(\text{customer} \bowtie \sigma_{\text{product} = \text{"car"}}(\text{order}))
$$

#### (iii) Details of all customers who purchased "phone" priced under \$300

1. First we filter `order` for rows where `product = "phone"` and `price < 300`:

$$
T_{\text{target\_orders}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{product} = \text{"phone"} \land \text{price} < 300}(\text{order})
$$

2. Next we natural-join `customer` with $T_{\text{target\_orders}}$ on `id`:

$$
\text{customer} \bowtie T_{\text{target\_orders}}
$$

To return only the customer profile columns (`id, name, age, gender`), we project those four columns:

$$
\Pi_{\text{customer.id}, \text{name}, \text{age}, \text{gender}}(\text{customer} \bowtie T_{\text{target\_orders}})
$$

### 🎯 GQ2 · 2024 Q.2(b) · Employees Working on Multiple Projects

> **Appeared:** 2024 Q.2(b) — (4 marks, CO1)

**Question:**

EMPLOYEE (EID, Name, Department, Salary)  
PROJECT (PID, Name, Budget)  
WORKS_ON (EID, PID, Hours)  
Suppose a company wants to find employees working on multiple projects. Write the relational algebra expression for it.

*(Sorting Note: Uses $\gamma$ aggregation, see T04.4)*

**Full Answer:**

An employee is working on multiple projects if their `EID` is linked to two or more distinct `PID` values in `WORKS_ON`.

#### Method 1: Extended Relational Algebra via Aggregation (Standard)

1. First we group `WORKS_ON` by `EID` using $\gamma$ and count how many project IDs (`PID`) each employee works on:

$$
T_{\text{counts}} \leftarrow {}_{\text{EID}}\gamma_{\text{count}(\text{PID}) \ \text{as} \ \text{proj\_count}}(\text{WORKS\_ON})
$$

2. Next we filter $T_{\text{counts}}$ using $\sigma$ to keep only employees with `proj_count > 1`:

$$
T_{\text{multi}} \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{proj\_count} > 1}(T_{\text{counts}})
$$

3. Finally, we natural-join `EMPLOYEE` with $T_{\text{multi}}$ on `EID` and project `EID` and `Name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{EID}, \text{Name}}(\text{EMPLOYEE} \bowtie T_{\text{multi}})
$$

#### Method 2: Pure Relational Algebra via Self-Join (Classical Alternative)

1. First we use $\rho$ to create two renamed copies of `WORKS_ON`, called $W_1$ and $W_2$:

$$
W_1 \leftarrow \rho_{W_1}(\text{WORKS\_ON})
$$

$$
W_2 \leftarrow \rho_{W_2}(\text{WORKS\_ON})
$$

2. Next we take the Cartesian product $W_1 \times W_2$ and filter for pairs that belong to the same employee ($W_1.\text{EID} = W_2.\text{EID}$) working on two different projects ($W_1.\text{PID} \neq W_2.\text{PID}$):

$$
T_{\text{pairs}} \leftarrow \sigma_{W_1.\text{EID} = W_2.\text{EID} \land W_1.\text{PID} \neq W_2.\text{PID}}(W_1 \times W_2)
$$

3. Finally, we project the employee IDs from $T_{\text{pairs}}$, natural-join with `EMPLOYEE`, and project `EID` and `Name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{EID}, \text{Name}}(\text{EMPLOYEE} \bowtie \Pi_{W_1.\text{EID}}(T_{\text{pairs}}))
$$

### 🎯 GQ3 · CT-01 Q.04(v–viii) · University Student, Course, Alumni Schema Drills

> **Appeared:** CT-01 Q.04(v–viii) — (4 marks, CO1)

**Question:**

Relations: $\text{STUDENT}(\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}, \text{Dept\_Name}, \text{CGPA})$, $\text{COURSE}(\text{Course\_ID}, \text{Course\_Name}, \text{Student\_ID}, \text{Semester}, \text{Marks})$, $\text{ALUMNI}(\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}, \text{Dept\_Name})$.  
Write the relational algebra expressions for the following conditions using the relations above:  
**(v)** Rename the relation $\text{STUDENT}$ as $\text{S}$ and display the $\text{Student\_ID}$ and $\text{Name}$ of all students.  
**(vi)** Retrieve the $\text{Student\_ID}$, $\text{Name}$, and $\text{Course\_Name}$ of all students who belong to the CSE department and scored 80 or more marks in any course.  
**(vii)** Retrieve the $\text{Student\_ID}$ and $\text{Name}$ of all students who are either in the $\text{STUDENT}$ relation or the $\text{ALUMNI}$ relation.  
**(viii)** Determine the number of students in each department.  
*(Note: Sub-parts are numbered v, vi, vii, viii on the original question script; part viii uses $\gamma$ aggregation, see T04.4).*

**Full Answer:**

#### (v) Rename relation STUDENT as S and display Student_ID and Name

First we rename `STUDENT` to `S` using $\rho_S$, and then we project `Student_ID` and `Name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}}(\rho_S(\text{STUDENT}))
$$

#### (vi) Student_ID, Name, and Course_Name for CSE students with Marks $\ge 80$

1. First we filter `STUDENT` using $\sigma$ for students in the `"CSE"` department:

$$
T_1 \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{Dept\_Name} = \text{"CSE"}}(\text{STUDENT})
$$

2. Next we filter `COURSE` using $\sigma$ for course enrollments where `Marks >= 80`:

$$
T_2 \leftarrow \sigma_{\text{Marks} \ge 80}(\text{COURSE})
$$

3. Finally, we natural-join $T_1$ and $T_2$ on `Student_ID` and project `Student_ID`, `Name`, and `Course_Name`:

$$
\Pi_{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}, \text{Course\_Name}}(T_1 \bowtie T_2)
$$

#### (vii) Student_ID and Name of students in either STUDENT or ALUMNI

Because `STUDENT` has 4 columns and `ALUMNI` has 3 columns, we cannot union them directly. First we project both tables down to `(Student_ID, Name)` so they become union-compatible, and then we combine them with $\cup$:

$$
\Pi_{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}}(\text{STUDENT}) \cup \Pi_{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Name}}(\text{ALUMNI})
$$

#### (viii) Number of students in each department

We group `STUDENT` by `Dept_Name` using $\gamma$ and count `Student_ID` for each department:

$$
{}_{\text{Dept\_Name}}\gamma_{\text{count}(\text{Student\_ID})}(\text{STUDENT})
$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Check Union Compatibility Before Every Set Operation:** Whenever you see the word "either ... or ...", do not write $R \cup S$ directly. Check whether $R$ and $S$ have identical column counts. If not, project the common target columns first.
2. **Numeric Comparisons Do Not Need Quotes:** In queries filtering by price or marks (such as $\text{price} < 300$ or $\text{Marks} \ge 80$), never write strings like `price < "300"`. Write numbers as bare numeric literals.
3. **Remember the Inequality in Self-Joins:** When using self-joins to detect multiple occurrences, writing $W_1.\text{PID} \neq W_2.\text{PID}$ is mandatory. Writing $W_1.\text{PID} = W_2.\text{PID}$ matches each row with itself, returning every single employee.
4. **Natural Join Equates All Matching Names:** In CT-01 Q.04(vi), both `STUDENT` and `COURSE` contain `Student_ID`. The natural join $\bowtie$ automatically matches on this key.
5. **Always State Foreign Key Assumptions:** In schemas where the foreign key name differs or is not formally underlined (such as `order.id` referencing `customer.id`), write a brief assumption line stating that `order.id` represents the customer identifier.
