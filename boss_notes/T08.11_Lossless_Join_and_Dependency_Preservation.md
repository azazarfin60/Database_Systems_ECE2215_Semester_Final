# T08.11: Lossless-Join Decomposition and Dependency Preservation

> **Topic:** T08 — Normalization & Functional Dependencies | **Priority:** 🟡 | **Exam Frequency:** 0/7 finals (0 marks)
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 7 (§7.1.2, §7.2.3, §7.4.4, §7.5) · Lecture Deck 06 (Slides 52–62) · Classnote by Raidah (pp. 24–25)

## Why This Topic Matters

Whenever we normalize a database, we split large tables into smaller ones. However, you cannot just slice a table in half at random! Every valid decomposition must pass two strict safety checks. First, when you join the smaller tables back together, you must get back the exact original rows without inventing fake "spurious" rows (**lossless-join property**). Second, you should be able to check every functional dependency rule inside a single table without having to join tables together on every `INSERT` or `UPDATE` (**dependency preservation property**). In RUET exams, this topic tests how to check both properties using attribute closures ($X^+$) and why 3NF and BCNF differ on dependency preservation.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Lossless Decomposition:** "We say that the decomposition is a **lossless decomposition** if there is no loss of information by replacing $R$ with two relation schemas $R_1$ and $R_2$." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.1.2 (p. 307)

> **Lossless-Join Condition:** "$R_1$ and $R_2$ form a lossless decomposition of $R$ if at least one of the following functional dependencies is in $F^+$:
> - $R_1 \cap R_2 \rightarrow R_1$
> - $R_1 \cap R_2 \rightarrow R_2$" — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.2.3 (p. 312)

> **Dependency-Preserving Decomposition:** "Let $F' = F_1 \cup F_2 \cup \cdots \cup F_n$. $F'$ is a set of functional dependencies on schema $R$, but, in general, $F' \neq F$. However, even if $F' \neq F$, it may be that $F'^+ = F^+$. If the latter is true, then every dependency in $F$ is logically implied by $F'$, and, if we verify that $F'$ is satisfied, we have verified that $F$ is satisfied. We say that a decomposition having the property $F'^+ = F^+$ is a **dependency-preserving decomposition**." — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.4.4 (p. 329)

> **Spurious Tuples:** *(Course definition)* Erroneous, phantom tuples generated during the natural join of decomposed relations that do not exist in the original relation and represent false associations.

## Concepts of Relation Decomposition

Decomposing a relation schema $R$ means splitting its columns across two or more smaller tables:

$$
D = \{R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_k\} \quad \text{such that} \quad R = R_1 \cup R_2 \cup \cdots \cup R_k
$$

The goal is to replace one messy table that has anomalies with smaller, well-organized tables (Lecture Deck 06 Slide 52).

```text
                        Relation Decomposition
                                   │
                  ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
                  ▼                                 ▼
      Lossless-Join Decomposition           Lossy Decomposition
   (Exact original relation restored)    (Spurious tuples generated)
```

Depending on how we choose the overlapping columns between the tables, every decomposition falls into one of two buckets:
1. **Lossless-Join Decomposition:** Joining the split tables back together reproduces the exact original rows, with nothing missing and nothing fake added.
2. **Lossy Decomposition:** Joining the split tables back together creates extra fake rows (**spurious tuples**), making it impossible to tell which rows were real.

## Lossless-Join Decomposition in Depth

### Why Does "More Rows" Mean "Loss of Information"? (Intuitive Analogy)

Suppose two students, **Alice** and **Bob**, are both in the **ECE** department. Alice works on project **P1**, and Bob works on project **P2**:

| Student | Dept | Project |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| Alice | ECE | P1 |
| Bob | ECE | P2 |

Now suppose someone splits this table on `Dept` into $R_1(\text{Student}, \text{Dept})$ and $R_2(\text{Dept}, \text{Project})$. Look at what happens when we join $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ on `Dept = ECE`:
- Because both Alice and Bob match `ECE`, and `ECE` matches both `P1` and `P2`, the natural join produces **4 rows**: `(Alice, ECE, P1)`, **`(Alice, ECE, P2)`**, **`(Bob, ECE, P1)`**, and `(Bob, ECE, P2)`!
- Even though we got *more* rows back (4 instead of 2), we **lost information** because we can no longer tell which student actually worked on which project! The two fake combinations `(Alice, ECE, P2)` and `(Bob, ECE, P1)` are called **spurious tuples**.
- Why did this happen? Because `Dept` (`ECE`) was **not a unique key** in either $R_1$ or $R_2$.

*(Note: The full 4-tuple Lecture Deck 06 Slide 61–62 table trace $R(X, Y, Z)$ producing 10 rows is solved step by step in **LQ2** below.)*

### Relational Algebra Formulation

Formally, if we split relation schema $R$ into $R_1$ and $R_2$, the decomposition is **lossless** if and only if for every valid table instance $r(R)$:

$$
\Pi_{R_1}(r) \bowtie \Pi_{R_2}(r) = r
$$

On the other hand, if the natural join produces extra spurious tuples on top of the original rows:

$$
r \subset \Pi_{R_1}(r) \bowtie \Pi_{R_2}(r)
$$

then the decomposition is **lossy** (Silberschatz 7e §7.1.2).

## Mathematical Test for Lossless-Join Decomposition

Instead of inventing sample rows and joining them by hand every time, we can use functional dependencies to test whether a split is lossless in seconds (Silberschatz 7e §7.2.3, Lecture Deck 06 Slide 55).

### Why the Superkey Rule Works

Think about how a foreign-key join works in SQL: when you join an `EMPLOYEE` table with a `DEPARTMENT` table on `Dept_ID`, why doesn't it create fake rows? Because `Dept_ID` is the **primary key (a superkey)** of the `DEPARTMENT` table, so each employee row matches **at most one** department row!

That gives us the golden rule for a binary decomposition into $R_1$ and $R_2$: **the overlapping attributes $(R_1 \cap R_2)$ must be a superkey of $R_1$ or a superkey of $R_2$**:

$$
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1 \in F^+ \quad \text{OR} \quad (R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2 \in F^+
$$

### 3-Step Exam Recipe (Using Attribute Closure)

To check if splitting $R$ into $R_1$ and $R_2$ is lossless:
1. Find the overlapping columns: $K = R_1 \cap R_2$.
2. Compute the attribute closure $K^+$ using the given functional dependencies $F$.
3. Check if $K^+$ contains all columns of $R_1$ ($R_1 \subseteq K^+$) **or** all columns of $R_2$ ($R_2 \subseteq K^+$). If either holds, the split is **lossless**. Otherwise, it is **lossy**.

### Worked Example: Applying the Lossless Test

Consider relation schema:

$$
R(A, B, C) \quad \text{with} \quad F = \{A \to B\}
$$

Suppose $R$ is decomposed into:

$$
R_1(A, B) \quad \text{and} \quad R_2(A, C)
$$

**Step-by-step verification:**
1. Find intersection: $R_1 \cap R_2 = \{A, B\} \cap \{A, C\} = \{A\}$.
2. Compute closure: $A^+ = \{A, B\}$ (since $A \to B$).
3. Compare with sub-relations:
   - $R_1 = \{A, B\}$. Because $R_1 \subseteq A^+$, the dependency $A \to R_1$ holds.
4. Because the common attribute $A$ forms a superkey of $R_1$, the decomposition is **Lossless** (Lecture Deck 06 Slide 56; Raidah p. 24).

## Dependency Preservation in Depth

### Why Dependency Preservation Matters (Intuitive Explanation)

Every functional dependency $\alpha \to \beta$ is a business rule that the database must enforce whenever someone inserts or updates a row.
- If all the columns of $\alpha \to \beta$ live together inside one table $R_i$, the DBMS can check that rule instantly using a simple key or `UNIQUE` index on $R_i$.
- However, if splitting the table separates $\alpha$ into $R_1$ and $\beta$ into $R_2$, the DBMS cannot check $\alpha \to \beta$ by looking at $R_1$ or $R_2$ alone! Instead, it would have to join $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ every single time a row is inserted.

When all original FDs can still be checked locally within individual tables (without needing any joins), we say the decomposition is **dependency preserving** (Silberschatz 7e §7.3.1.2).

### Formal Definition of Dependency Preservation

Suppose we decompose schema $R$ (with functional dependencies $F$) into $D = \{R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_k\}$:

1. For each table $R_i$, we collect all functional dependencies in $F^+$ whose columns lie completely inside $R_i$. This local set is called the **restriction** of $F$ to $R_i$, written $F_i$:
   
$$
F_i = \{\alpha \to \beta \in F^+ \mid \alpha \cup \beta \subseteq R_i\}
$$

2. Next, we combine the local rules from all the split tables into one set $F'$:
   
$$
F' = F_1 \cup F_2 \cup \cdots \cup F_k
$$

3. The decomposition $D$ is **dependency preserving** if and only if those local rules imply the exact same closure as the original set $F$:
   
$$
F'^+ = F^+
$$

In plain English, enforcing the local FDs on each table separately automatically enforces every FD in $F$.

---

## Testing for Dependency Preservation

### Practical Sufficient Test

If every functional dependency $\alpha \to \beta \in F$ has all its attributes contained within at least one decomposed relation $R_i$:

$$
(\alpha \cup \beta) \subseteq R_i
$$

then the decomposition is **immediately guaranteed** to be dependency preserving.

### General Polynomial Test (Silberschatz 7e §7.4.4)

When an FD in $F$ spans multiple decomposed relations, it might still be preserved indirectly through implied transitivity. To test whether $\alpha \to \beta$ is preserved without computing $F^+$, run this algorithm:

```text
Input: Functional dependency α → β, decomposition D = {R1, R2, ..., Rk}, FD set F
Output: True if α → β is preserved, False otherwise

result := α;
repeat
    for each Ri in D do
    begin
        t := ((result ∩ Ri)+ ∩ Ri);
        result := result ∪ t;
    end;
until (result does not change);

if (β ⊆ result) then
    return True;   // α → β is preserved
else
    return False;  // α → β is lost
```

If all dependencies $\alpha \to \beta \in F$ pass this test, the decomposition is dependency preserving.

## Contrasting Dependency Preservation Examples

### Case 1: Dependency Preserving Decomposition

Consider relation $R(A, B, C)$ with:

$$
F = \{A \to B, \; B \to C\}
$$

Decompose $R$ into:

$$
R_1(A, B) \quad \text{and} \quad R_2(B, C)
$$

- In $R_1(A, B)$: Attributes $\{A, B\} \subseteq R_1$, so $A \to B$ can be enforced locally.
- In $R_2(B, C)$: Attributes $\{B, C\} \subseteq R_2$, so $B \to C$ can be enforced locally.
- Every dependency in $F$ is enforced within a single table.

Therefore, the decomposition is **Dependency Preserving** (Lecture Deck 06 Slide 57).

### Case 2: Non-Dependency Preserving Decomposition

Consider the same relation $R(A, B, C)$ and $F = \{A \to B, \; B \to C\}$.

Suppose we decompose $R$ into:

$$
R_1(A, B) \quad \text{and} \quad R_2(A, C)
$$

- In $R_1(A, B)$: $A \to B$ can be enforced locally.
- In $R_2(A, C)$: Neither $B$ nor $C$ both exist in $R_2$. The dependency $B \to C$ cannot be checked in $R_1$ or $R_2$.
- Checking $B \to C$ requires computing the join $R_1 \bowtie R_2$.

Therefore, the decomposition is **NOT Dependency Preserving** (Lecture Deck 06 Slide 58).

## 3NF vs. BCNF: The Decomposition Guarantees

The trade-off between 3NF and BCNF centers directly on decomposition properties (Lecture Deck 06 Slide 59; Silberschatz 7e §7.3.3).

| Property | Third Normal Form (3NF) | Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Lossless-Join** | Always guaranteed | Always guaranteed |
| **Dependency Preservation** | **Always guaranteed** (via 3NF synthesis) | **Not always guaranteed** |
| **Redundancy Removal** | Retains some redundancy when overlapping keys exist | Removes all functional dependency redundancy |
| **Standard Algorithm** | 3NF Synthesis (using Canonical Cover $F_c$) | BCNF Decomposition (top-down splitting) |

```text
3NF Design:   [ Lossless Join: YES ]  AND  [ Dependency Preservation: YES ]
BCNF Design:  [ Lossless Join: YES ]  BUT  [ Dependency Preservation: MAY FAIL ]
```

When a database architect must choose between 3NF and BCNF:
- Choose **3NF** if enforcing business rules without join penalties is critical.
- Choose **BCNF** if eliminating all functional dependency redundancy and update anomalies is the top priority.

## Worked Example: Step-by-Step Decomposition Drill

This complete problem comes from Classnote by Raidah (p. 25).

### Problem Statement

Given relation schema:

$$
R(A, B, C, D, E, F)
$$

with functional dependencies:

$$
F = \{AB \to C, \; C \to DE, \; E \to F, \; F \to A\}
$$

1. Find all candidate keys of $R$.
2. Identify the normal form violation.
3. Propose a lossless-join decomposition into two relations and verify its properties.

### Step 1: Candidate Key Calculation

Compute attribute closures:
- $(AB)^+ = \{A, B, C, D, E, F\} \implies AB$ is a candidate key.
- $(BC)^+ = \{B, C, D, E, F, A\} \implies BC$ is a candidate key.
- $(BE)^+ = \{B, E, F, A, C, D\} \implies BE$ is a candidate key.
- $(BF)^+ = \{B, F, A, C, D, E\} \implies BF$ is a candidate key.

Candidate keys of $R$:

$$
\text{Candidate Keys} = \{AB, BC, BE, BF\}
$$

- **Prime Attributes:** $\{A, B, C, E, F\}$
- **Non-prime Attribute:** $\{D\}$

### Step 2: Identify Anomalies

Examine dependency $C \to DE$:
- By decomposition rule: $C \to D$ and $C \to E$.
- Attribute $D$ is non-prime. Attribute $C$ is prime, but $C$ alone is not a candidate key (only a proper subset of $BC$).
- Therefore, $C \to D$ represents a transitive dependency where a non-superkey determines a non-prime attribute.
- This violates 2NF, 3NF, and BCNF.

### Step 3: Step-by-Step Lossless Decomposition

Decompose $R$ based on the violating dependency $C \to D$:

$$
R_1(A, B, C, E, F) \quad \text{and} \quad R_2(C, D)
$$

Let us verify the decomposition properties:

#### 1. Lossless-Join Test
- Find intersection: $R_1 \cap R_2 = \{C\}$.
- Compute closure of intersection: $C^+ = \{C, D, E, F, A\}$.
- Compare with $R_2$: $R_2 = \{C, D\} \subseteq C^+$.
- Because $C \to R_2$, common attribute $C$ forms a superkey of $R_2$.
- **Result:** The decomposition is strictly **Lossless**.

#### 2. Dependency Preservation Check
- In $R_2(C, D)$: The dependency $C \to D$ is preserved locally.
- In $R_1(A, B, C, E, F)$: The dependencies $AB \to C$, $C \to E$, $E \to F$, and $F \to A$ involve only attributes within $R_1$. They are all preserved locally.
- **Result:** The decomposition is **Dependency Preserving**.

## 🔮 Likely Questions

### 🎯 LQ1 · State and Prove the Necessary and Sufficient Condition for a Binary Decomposition to be Lossless-Join

> **Likely Question:** High-Probability Exam Problem — (5 marks)

**Question:**

State the condition for a binary relation decomposition to be lossless-join. Prove why the condition ensures that no information is lost during natural join.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Condition for Lossless-Join Decomposition

Let a relation schema $R$ be decomposed into two schemas $R_1$ and $R_2$ such that $R = R_1 \cup R_2$. The decomposition is a **lossless-join decomposition** with respect to a set $F$ of functional dependencies if and only if at least one of the following dependencies is in $F^+$:

$$
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1 \quad \text{OR} \quad (R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2
$$

That is, the intersection attributes must form a superkey of $R_1$ or a superkey of $R_2$.

#### 2. Proof Sketch

Let $r$ be a legal relation on $R$, and let $r_1 = \Pi_{R_1}(r)$ and $r_2 = \Pi_{R_2}(r)$. By definition of natural join, every tuple $t \in r$ appears in $r_1 \bowtie r_2$. Therefore:

$$
r \subseteq r_1 \bowtie r_2
$$

We must now prove that if $(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1$, then no spurious tuples exist, meaning $r_1 \bowtie r_2 \subseteq r$.

1. Suppose $(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1$ holds.
2. Let $t = (t[R_1 \cap R_2], t[R_1 - R_2], t[R_2 - R_1])$ be any tuple in $r_1 \bowtie r_2$.
3. By definition of natural join, there must exist:
   - A tuple $u \in r$ such that $u[R_1] = t[R_1]$, meaning $u[R_1 \cap R_2] = t[R_1 \cap R_2]$.
   - A tuple $v \in r$ such that $v[R_2] = t[R_2]$, meaning $v[R_1 \cap R_2] = t[R_1 \cap R_2]$.
4. Because $u[R_1 \cap R_2] = v[R_1 \cap R_2]$ and the functional dependency $(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1$ holds on $r$, tuples $u$ and $v$ must have identical values on all attributes of $R_1$:
   
$$
u[R_1] = v[R_1]
$$

5. Since $v$ belongs to $r$, and $v$ matches $u$ on $R_1$ and matches $t$ on $R_2$:
   
$$
v = (v[R_1], v[R_2 - R_1]) = (t[R_1], t[R_2 - R_1]) = t
$$

6. Thus, tuple $t$ is identical to $v$, which is already in $r$.
7. No spurious tuples can exist. Therefore:

$$
r_1 \bowtie r_2 = r
$$

The decomposition is strictly lossless.

### 🎯 LQ2 · What are Spurious Tuples? Demonstrate with a Concrete Numerical Example Why a Decomposition Can Be Lossy

> **Likely Question:** High-Probability Exam Problem — (5 marks)

**Question:**

Explain the term "spurious tuples". Using a sample table instance, demonstrate how an improper decomposition generates spurious tuples and destroys information.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. What Are Spurious Tuples?

Spurious tuples are extra, invalid records generated when two decomposed tables are joined together on non-key attributes. They do not exist in the original relation and represent false associations. A decomposition that produces spurious tuples is called **lossy** because the database cannot identify which associations represent genuine real-world facts.

#### 2. Concrete Example

Consider the relation schema $R(X, Y, Z)$ with no functional dependency where $X$ determines $Y$ or $Z$.

Suppose relation $r(R)$ contains the following 4 tuples:

| X | Y | Z |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| X1 | Y1 | Z1 |
| X2 | Y1 | Z1 |
| X1 | Y2 | Z2 |
| X1 | Y3 | Z3 |

#### 3. Decomposing into $R_1(X, Y)$ and $R_2(X, Z)$

Project $r$ onto $R_1$ and $R_2$:

- **$r_1 = \Pi_{X, Y}(r)$:** contains $\{(\text{X1}, \text{Y1}), (\text{X2}, \text{Y1}), (\text{X1}, \text{Y2}), (\text{X1}, \text{Y3})\}$ (4 rows)
- **$r_2 = \Pi_{X, Z}(r)$:** contains $\{(\text{X1}, \text{Z1}), (\text{X2}, \text{Z1}), (\text{X1}, \text{Z2}), (\text{X1}, \text{Z3})\}$ (4 rows)

#### 4. Natural Join $r_1 \bowtie r_2$

Joining on common attribute $X$:
- For $X = \text{X2}$: produces 1 row: $(\text{X2}, \text{Y1}, \text{Z1})$.
- For $X = \text{X1}$: $r_1$ has 3 rows with $X = \text{X1}$, and $r_2$ has 3 rows with $X = \text{X1}$. Their Cartesian combination produces $3 \times 3 = 9$ rows.

Total rows in $r_1 \bowtie r_2 = 1 + 9 = 10$ rows:

| X | Y | Z | Nature of Record |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| X1 | Y1 | Z1 | Original tuple |
| **X1** | **Y1** | **Z2** | **Spurious tuple** |
| **X1** | **Y1** | **Z3** | **Spurious tuple** |
| X2 | Y1 | Z1 | Original tuple |
| **X1** | **Y2** | **Z1** | **Spurious tuple** |
| X1 | Y2 | Z2 | Original tuple |
| **X1** | **Y2** | **Z3** | **Spurious tuple** |
| **X1** | **Y3** | **Z1** | **Spurious tuple** |
| **X1** | **Y3** | **Z2** | **Spurious tuple** |
| X1 | Y3 | Z3 | Original tuple |

The 6 marked tuples are spurious. The database cannot determine whether $(\text{X1}, \text{Y1}, \text{Z2})$ was a real relationship or an artifact of the join. Information has been lost.

### 🎯 LQ3 · Define Dependency Preservation. Explain Why 3NF Always Guarantees Dependency Preservation Whereas BCNF May Not

> **Likely Question:** High-Probability Exam Problem — (5 marks)

**Question:**

Define dependency preservation in relational database decomposition. Why is it always possible to achieve a dependency-preserving decomposition in 3NF, but not always in BCNF? Illustrate with an example.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Definition of Dependency Preservation

A decomposition $D = \{R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_k\}$ of a relation schema $R$ with functional dependency set $F$ is **dependency preserving** if:

$$
(F_1 \cup F_2 \cup \cdots \cup F_k)^+ = F^+
$$

where $F_i$ is the restriction of $F^+$ to relation $R_i$. This ensures that every functional dependency in $F$ can be enforced by examining individual decomposed relations without computing joins.

#### 2. Why 3NF Always Guarantees Dependency Preservation

Third Normal Form includes a relaxation condition: in every non-trivial dependency $\alpha \to \beta$, either $\alpha$ is a superkey OR every attribute in $\beta - \alpha$ is a prime attribute.

Because of this condition, Bernstein's 3NF synthesis algorithm can take a **canonical cover** $F_c$ of $F$ and directly convert each dependency $\alpha \to \beta \in F_c$ into a relation schema $R_i = \alpha \cup \beta$. Because each dependency in $F_c$ is housed inside its own relation, every functional dependency is preserved automatically.

#### 3. Why BCNF May Sacrifice Dependency Preservation

BCNF forbids the prime-attribute relaxation. It insists that every determinant must be a superkey without exception.

When a schema contains overlapping candidate keys, enforcing BCNF can force the decomposition to break apart the attributes of a functional dependency across separate relations.

**Counterexample:**
Consider relation $R(\text{Student}, \text{Course}, \text{Instructor})$ with functional dependencies:
1. $(\text{Student}, \text{Course}) \to \text{Instructor}$
2. $\text{Instructor} \to \text{Course}$

Candidate keys: $\{(\text{Student}, \text{Course}), (\text{Student}, \text{Instructor})\}$.

- The schema is in 3NF because in $\text{Instructor} \to \text{Course}$, $\text{Course}$ is a prime attribute.
- The schema violates BCNF because $\text{Instructor}$ is not a superkey.
- To reach BCNF, we decompose using $\text{Instructor} \to \text{Course}$ into:
  - $R_1(\text{Instructor}, \text{Course})$
  - $R_2(\text{Student}, \text{Instructor})$

Both $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in BCNF. However, the original dependency $(\text{Student}, \text{Course}) \to \text{Instructor}$ cannot be tested in $R_1$ or $R_2$ alone because no table contains both $\text{Student}$ and $\text{Course}$ alongside $\text{Instructor}$. Checking this dependency requires computing $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ on every insert. Dependency preservation has been lost.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **State the superkey condition clearly:** For a binary decomposition to be lossless, $(R_1 \cap R_2)$ must determine either all of $R_1$ or all of $R_2$. Determining just one column is not enough.
2. **Remember that "lossy" means MORE tuples:** A lossy decomposition does not lose rows when you join the tables back together. Instead, it creates extra fake (spurious) rows, which destroys our ability to tell which relationships are real.
3. **Use the 3NF synthesis vs. BCNF contrast:** Whenever an exam question asks about the trade-off between 3NF and BCNF, point out that 3NF always guarantees both lossless join and dependency preservation, whereas BCNF guarantees a lossless join but can lose dependency preservation.
4. **Always compute attribute closure to verify a lossless join:** Do not just guess by inspection. Write out the closure $(R_1 \cap R_2)^+$ step by step to earn full marks.
