# T08.6: Normal Forms — 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF

> **Topic:** T08 — Normalization & Functional Dependencies | **Priority:** 🟠 | **Exam Frequency:** 3/7 finals (+ CT-03) (14 marks)
> **Sources:** Silberschatz 7e Ch. 7 (§7.3, §7.7, §7.8) · Lecture Deck 06 (Slides 3–4, 30–40) · Raidah Notes (p. 22–23)

## Why This Topic Matters

Normal forms give us a step-by-step checklist for judging whether a database table is well designed or full of hidden traps. RUET semester finals frequently ask you to define 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF and illustrate their rules with small examples. Getting comfortable with these three normal forms makes theory questions easy marks and prepares you for full table-decomposition problems in the next notes.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Atomic Domain & First Normal Form (1NF):** "A domain is **atomic** if elements of the domain are considered to be indivisible units. We say that a relation schema $R$ is in **first normal form** (1NF) if the domains of all attributes of $R$ are atomic."
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.8 (p. 342)

> **Second Normal Form (2NF):** "A functional dependency $\alpha \rightarrow \beta$ is called a partial dependency if there is a proper subset $\gamma$ of $\alpha$ such that $\gamma \rightarrow \beta$; we say that $\beta$ is partially dependent on $\alpha$. A relation schema $R$ is in **second normal form** (2NF) if each attribute $A$ in $R$ meets one of the following criteria:
> - It appears in a candidate key.
> - It is not partially dependent on a candidate key."
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.7 / Practice Exercise 7.19 (pp. 356–357)

> **Third Normal Form (3NF):** "A relation schema $R$ is in **third normal form** (3NF) with respect to a set $F$ of functional dependencies if, for all functional dependencies in $F^+$ of the form $\alpha \rightarrow \beta$, where $\alpha \subseteq R$ and $\beta \subseteq R$, at least one of the following holds:
> - $\alpha \rightarrow \beta$ is a trivial functional dependency.
> - $\alpha$ is a superkey for $R$.
> - Each attribute $A$ in $\beta - \alpha$ is contained in a candidate key for $R$."
> — Silberschatz, Korth & Sudarshan, *Database System Concepts* (7th Ed.), §7.3.2 (p. 317)

> **Normalization (Course Definition):** "Normalization is the process of organizing data in a database to:
> - Reduce unnecessary data redundancy
> - Eliminate undesirable data dependencies
> - Prevent insertion, deletion and update anomalies
> - Improve data consistency and integrity"
> — Lecture Deck 06, Slide 4 *(Course definition)*

## Understanding Normalization

Normalization is a structured design process that takes a bloated, error-prone table and splits it into smaller, clean tables that can be joined back together without losing any data.

### The Objectives of Normalization

1. **Minimize Data Redundancy:** Stop storing the same fact across dozens of rows.
2. **Prevent Anomalies:** Eliminate update anomalies, insertion anomalies, and deletion anomalies.
3. **Ensure Data Integrity:** Keep records consistent whenever a value changes.
4. **Preserve Dependencies:** Enforce real-world constraints cleanly without needing expensive multi-table joins.

### The Cumulative Hierarchy

Think of normal forms as Russian nesting dolls or levels in a game. You cannot reach a higher normal form until you have satisfied all the levels below it:

```text
Unnormalized Form (UNF)
       │
       ▼ (Eliminate multi-valued attributes and repeating groups)
First Normal Form (1NF)
       │
       ▼ (Eliminate partial functional dependencies)
Second Normal Form (2NF)
       │
       ▼ (Eliminate transitive functional dependencies)
Third Normal Form (3NF)
       │
       ▼ (Eliminate non-trivial FDs where determinant is not a superkey)
Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)
```

Because this hierarchy is cumulative, every table in 3NF is automatically in 2NF, and every table in 2NF is automatically in 1NF.

---

## First Normal Form (1NF)

**Intuition:** 1NF is the "one value per box" rule. Database queries become messy and slow if you cram comma-separated lists or nested mini-tables inside a single cell. So a relation is in 1NF if and only if every column holds **atomic** (indivisible) values.

### Rules of 1NF

1. Every cell at the intersection of a row and column must hold a single scalar value.
2. Multi-valued attributes (such as comma-separated phone numbers or lists) are not allowed.
3. Repeating groups of columns (like `Course1, Course2, Course3`) are not allowed.
4. Every row must be uniquely identifiable by a primary key.

### Concrete Example: 1NF Violation and Fix

Consider an unnormalized student contact table:

| Student_ID | Student_Name | Phone_Number |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| S01 | Fahim | 01711, 01822 |
| S02 | Noor | 01933 |

Here, the cell for student `S01` packs two phone numbers into one box, which violates atomicity.

To bring this table into 1NF, we give each phone number its own row:

| Student_ID | Student_Name | Phone_Number |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| S01 | Fahim | 01711 |
| S01 | Fahim | 01822 |
| S02 | Noor | 01933 |

Now every cell holds a single atomic value, and the composite primary key for the 1NF table is `(Student_ID, Phone_Number)`.

---

## Second Normal Form (2NF)

**Intuition:** Once your table is in 1NF, look at any composite candidate key. Every non-key column in that table should be about the *whole* key, not just half of it. Second Normal Form (2NF) outlaws **partial functional dependencies** so you don't end up repeating facts about part of a key across many rows.

### Definitions and Key Terms

- **Prime Attribute:** An attribute that is part of at least one candidate key.
- **Non-Prime Attribute:** An attribute that does not belong to any candidate key.
- **Partial Functional Dependency:** When a non-prime attribute is determined by a proper subset (just a piece) of a composite candidate key.
- **Full Functional Dependency:** When a non-prime attribute requires the entire candidate key to determine it, and no smaller subset works.

### Rules of 2NF

1. The relation must already be in 1NF.
2. There must be no partial functional dependencies. In other words, every non-prime attribute must be fully functionally dependent on every candidate key.

> [!NOTE]
> If a 1NF table has only single-attribute candidate keys, it is **automatically in 2NF**. You cannot have a partial dependency when the key has only one attribute!

### Concrete Example: 2NF Violation and Fix

Consider the course enrollment relation:

$$
\text{ENROLL}(\text{StudentID}, \text{CourseID}, \text{StudentName}, \text{Grade})
$$

- Candidate Key: `(StudentID, CourseID)`
- Prime attributes: `StudentID`, `CourseID`
- Non-prime attributes: `StudentName`, `Grade`

Now look at the functional dependencies:
1. `(StudentID, CourseID) -> Grade` is a **full dependency** because you need both the student and the course to know the grade.
2. `StudentID -> StudentName` is a **partial dependency** because `StudentName` depends only on `StudentID` (part of the composite key), which causes `StudentName` to be repeated for every course that student takes.

Because `StudentID -> StudentName` is a partial dependency, `ENROLL` fails 2NF. To convert it to 2NF, we pull `StudentName` out into a separate table keyed by `StudentID`:
1. `STUDENT(StudentID, StudentName)` with Primary Key: `StudentID`.
2. `ENROLLMENT(StudentID, CourseID, Grade)` with Primary Key: `(StudentID, CourseID)`.

Both tables now satisfy 2NF.

---

## Third Normal Form (3NF)

**Intuition:** Even after you fix partial dependencies for 2NF, a non-key attribute might still piggyback on *another* non-key attribute (like `DeptName` depending on `DeptID` inside a `STUDENT` table). Third Normal Form (3NF) removes these **transitive functional dependencies** so that every non-key attribute depends directly on the key and nothing else.

### Transitive Functional Dependency

A transitive dependency happens when a non-prime attribute determines another non-prime attribute. Mathematically, if $X \rightarrow Y$ and $Y \rightarrow Z$ hold (where $X$ is a key, $Y$ is not a superkey, and $Z$ is non-prime), then $X \rightarrow Z$ is a transitive dependency through $Y$.

### Rules of 3NF

A relation is in 3NF if and only if:
1. It is already in 2NF.
2. It has no transitive dependencies. Formally, for every non-trivial functional dependency $X \rightarrow Y$, at least one of these two conditions must hold:
   - $X$ is a **superkey** of the relation, OR
   - Every attribute in $Y - X$ is a **prime attribute** (part of some candidate key).

*(Why does the formal definition allow $Y$ to be a prime attribute? Because if a non-key attribute happens to determine part of a secondary candidate key, that doesn't create a non-prime-to-non-prime transitive chain! That special case is allowed in 3NF and is only forbidden later in BCNF.)*

### Concrete Example: 3NF Violation and Fix

Consider the student department relation:

$$
\text{STUDENT\_DEPT}(\text{StudentID}, \text{StudentName}, \text{DeptID}, \text{DeptName})
$$

- Candidate Key: `StudentID` (since the key is a single attribute, this 1NF table is already in 2NF!)
- Prime attribute: `StudentID`
- Non-prime attributes: `StudentName`, `DeptID`, `DeptName`

Now inspect the dependencies:
1. `StudentID -> StudentName, DeptID` (fine, because `StudentID` is a superkey).
2. `DeptID -> DeptName` (violation, because `DeptID` is not a superkey and `DeptName` is not a prime attribute).

Here, `StudentID -> DeptID -> DeptName` forms a transitive chain, forcing us to repeat `DeptName` for every student in that department. To convert to 3NF, we move `DeptID -> DeptName` into its own department table:
1. `STUDENT(StudentID, StudentName, DeptID)` with Primary Key: `StudentID` and Foreign Key: `DeptID`.
2. `DEPARTMENT(DeptID, DeptName)` with Primary Key: `DeptID`.

Both relations now satisfy 3NF.

## Summary Comparison Table

| Normal Form | Required Prerequisite | Target Defect / Anomaly | Condition on Dependencies | Decomposition Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1NF** | Tabular structure | Non-atomic cells, multi-valued attributes, repeating groups | All attribute domains must contain atomic values | Flatten rows and separate repeated attributes into new tuples |
| **2NF** | In 1NF | Partial dependencies on composite keys | Every non-prime attribute must depend fully on each candidate key | Move partially dependent attributes and their determinant into a separate table |
| **3NF** | In 2NF | Transitive dependencies between non-prime attributes | For every $X \rightarrow Y$, $X$ is a superkey OR $Y$ is prime | Move transitively dependent attributes and their non-key determinant into a separate table |

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2017 Q.3(a) · Definition and Importance of Normalization

> **Appeared:** 2017 Q.3(a) — (03 Marks)

**Question:**

What do you mean by normalization? Why normalization is important for database?

*(Sorting Note: Also asks why normalization is important, cross-ref T08.1 / T08.10)*

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Definition of Normalization

**Normalization** is a systematic, formal process used in relational database design. It decomposes a relation schema $R$ into a set of smaller, well-structured relations $\{R_1, R_2, \dots, R_k\}$ to minimize data redundancy and eliminate modification anomalies while preserving data integrity and functional dependencies.

#### 2. Why Normalization is Important for Databases

Normalization is important for four primary reasons:

1. **Elimination of Data Redundancy:** Storing identical data values multiple times wastes secondary storage. Normalization ensures each fact is stored in exactly one place.
2. **Prevention of Modification Anomalies:**
   - *Insertion Anomaly:* Inability to add valid information about an entity without adding unrelated data.
   - *Deletion Anomaly:* Accidental loss of essential information when a single related record is deleted.
   - *Update Anomaly:* Inconsistent records resulting from updating a value in some tuples while missing others.
3. **Guaranteed Data Consistency:** Updates are applied to a single canonical record. This prevents contradictory records from existing in the database.
4. **Maintenance of Functional Dependencies:** Decomposition preserves business rules and integrity constraints so that database engines can enforce them efficiently.

### 🎯 GQ2 · 2017 Q.8(c) · Defining 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF

> **Appeared:** 2017 Q.8(c) — (03 Marks)

**Question:**

What is 1NF, 2NF and 3NF?

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. First Normal Form (1NF)

A relation schema $R$ is in **First Normal Form (1NF)** if all attribute domains contain only atomic (indivisible) values. Every row-and-column intersection contains exactly one single value. Multi-valued attributes, nested records, and repeating groups are strictly forbidden.

#### 2. Second Normal Form (2NF)

A relation schema $R$ is in **Second Normal Form (2NF)** if:
1. It is already in 1NF.
2. It contains **no partial functional dependencies**. Every non-prime attribute must depend fully on every candidate key. No non-prime attribute may depend on a proper subset of any candidate key.

#### 3. Third Normal Form (3NF)

A relation schema $R$ is in **Third Normal Form (3NF)** if:
1. It is already in 2NF.
2. It contains **no transitive dependencies**. Non-prime attributes must not depend on other non-prime attributes.
Formally, for every non-trivial functional dependency $X \rightarrow Y$ in $F^+$:
- $X$ is a superkey of $R$, OR
- Each attribute in $Y - X$ is a prime attribute (part of a candidate key).

### 🎯 GQ3 · 2018 Q.4(c) · Rules of 1NF and 2NF with Examples

> **Appeared:** 2018 Q.4(c) — (04 Marks)

**Question:**

Explain the rules of 1NF and 2NF with a proper example.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. First Normal Form (1NF) Rules and Example

**Rules:**
- All values stored in every cell must be atomic (indivisible units).
- Multi-valued attributes and composite attributes are disallowed.
- Each row must have a unique identifier (primary key).

**Example:**

*Violating 1NF (Multi-valued contact numbers):*

| Student_ID | Student_Name | Phone_List |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| S01 | Fahim | 01711, 01822 |
| S02 | Noor | 01933 |

*Converted to 1NF (Atomic cells):*

| Student_ID | Student_Name | Phone_Number |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| S01 | Fahim | 01711 |
| S01 | Fahim | 01822 |
| S02 | Noor | 01933 |

Primary Key: `(Student_ID, Phone_Number)`.

#### 2. Second Normal Form (2NF) Rules and Example

**Rules:**
- The relation must satisfy all conditions of 1NF.
- There must be no partial functional dependencies. Every non-prime attribute must depend on the full candidate key, not on a part of it.

**Example:**

Consider relation $\text{ENROLL}(\text{StudentID}, \text{CourseID}, \text{StudentName}, \text{Grade})$ with:
- Candidate Key: `(StudentID, CourseID)`
- FDs: `(StudentID, CourseID) -> Grade` and `StudentID -> StudentName`

Here, `StudentName` is non-prime and depends on `StudentID`, which is only part of the composite key. This is a partial dependency.

*Converted to 2NF (Decompose into two relations):*

1. **STUDENT**

| StudentID | StudentName |
| :---: | :--- |
| S01 | Fahim |
| S02 | Noor |

Primary Key: `StudentID`.

2. **ENROLLMENT**

| StudentID | CourseID | Grade |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| S01 | ECE2215 | A |
| S01 | ECE2201 | B+ |
| S02 | ECE2215 | A- |

Primary Key: `(StudentID, CourseID)`.

Both relations satisfy 2NF because all partial dependencies have been eliminated.

### 🎯 GQ4 · 2019 Q.3(a) · Normalization Concept with 1NF and 2NF Discussion

> **Appeared:** 2019 Q.3(a) — (04 Marks)

**Question:**

What is normalization? Discuss 1NF and 2NF.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. What is Normalization?

**Normalization** is a formal relational database design technique that organizes tables to minimize data redundancy and prevent insertion, update, and deletion anomalies. It decomposes relations with undesirable functional dependencies into smaller, well-structured relations that satisfy standard normal forms while guaranteeing lossless joins.

#### 2. Discussion of First Normal Form (1NF)

**Definition:** A relation is in 1NF if and only if every attribute domain contains atomic values and each cell holds a single value.

**Key Requirements:**
1. Atomicity: No lists, arrays, or composite records within a single table cell.
2. Distinct rows: A primary key must exist to uniquely identify each tuple.

**Illustration:**
If an employee table stores multiple skill tags like `"SQL, Python"` in a single column, it violates 1NF. Splitting each skill into a separate row with composite primary key `(Emp_ID, Skill)` achieves 1NF.

#### 3. Discussion of Second Normal Form (2NF)

**Definition:** A relation is in 2NF if it is in 1NF and contains no partial functional dependencies.

**Key Requirements:**
1. Cumulative: The relation must already satisfy 1NF.
2. Full dependency: Every non-prime attribute must depend on the whole candidate key. A proper subset of a composite candidate key cannot determine any non-prime attribute.
3. Single-attribute key shortcut: Any 1NF relation whose candidate keys consist of single attributes is automatically in 2NF.

**Illustration:**
In $\text{ORDER\_ITEM}(\underline{\text{OrderID}, \text{ItemID}}, \text{ItemName}, \text{Quantity})$, `ItemID -> ItemName` is a partial dependency because `ItemID` is a proper subset of the primary key. We decompose this into:
- $\text{ITEM}(\underline{\text{ItemID}}, \text{ItemName})$ in 2NF.
- $\text{ORDER\_LINE}(\underline{\text{OrderID}, \text{ItemID}}, \text{Quantity})$ in 2NF.

### 🎯 GQ5 · CT-03 Q.03 · Normalization Definition and Main Objective

> **Appeared:** CT-03 Q.03 — (03 Marks)

**Question:**

Define normalization and state its main objective. *(Also cross-referenced in T08.1)*

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Defining Normalization

**Normalization** is the systematic process of structuring a relational database schema through progressive decomposition of relations. It replaces anomalous relation schemas with smaller schemas satisfying precise normal form criteria, ensuring that all functional dependencies are preserved and decompositions are lossless.

#### 2. Main Objectives of Normalization

The primary objective of normalization is to design a database that is free from structural defects. Specifically, it aims to:

1. **Eliminate Modification Anomalies:** Ensure that insertions, updates, and deletions can occur without causing unintended side effects or loss of valid data.
2. **Minimize Data Redundancy:** Prevent the duplicate recording of information across tuples, saving storage space and avoiding inconsistency.
3. **Maintain Data Integrity and Consistency:** Ensure that every real-world fact is recorded in a single location so that updates reflect globally without conflicting values.
4. **Ensure Efficient Constraint Enforcement:** Make it easy to enforce functional dependencies and referential integrity directly through primary keys and foreign keys.

## 📖 High-Yield Exercises from the Book

### 🎯 HYE-01 · Exercise 7.22 · Lossless, Dependency-Preserving 3NF Synthesis

> **Source:** Silberschatz 7e · Chapter 7 (Exercise 7.22)  
> **Exam Relevance:** High-Probability Algorithmic Synthesis Drill  
> **Key Tested Concept:** Step-by-step Bernstein 3NF synthesis algorithm ensuring both lossless join and dependency preservation.

**Problem Statement:**
> Given relation schema $R = (A, B, C, D, E)$ and set of functional dependencies:
> $$
> F = \{A \rightarrow BC, \; CD \rightarrow E, \; B \rightarrow D, \; E \rightarrow A\}
> $$
> Apply the 3NF synthesis algorithm to obtain a lossless-join, dependency-preserving 3NF decomposition of $R$.

**Model Exam-Style Solution:**

1. **Step 1: Compute Canonical Cover $F_c$:**
   - From Exercise 7.7, the canonical cover for this exact FD set is:
     $$
     F_c = \{A \rightarrow BC, \; CD \rightarrow E, \; B \rightarrow D, \; E \rightarrow A\}
     $$
     *(All left-hand sides are minimal, and no extraneous attributes exist).*

2. **Step 2: Create a Relation for Each Dependency in $F_c$:**
   - For $A \rightarrow BC \implies R_1 = (A, B, C)$ with key $A$.
   - For $CD \rightarrow E \implies R_2 = (C, D, E)$ with key $CD$.
   - For $B \rightarrow D \implies R_3 = (B, D)$ with key $B$.
   - For $E \rightarrow A \implies R_4 = (E, A)$ with key $E$.

3. **Step 3: Candidate Key Check (Ensuring Lossless Join):**
   - The candidate keys of $R$ under $F$ are:
     $$
     \{A\}, \quad \{B, C\}, \quad \{C, D\}, \quad \{E\}
     $$
   - Check if any generated relation contains an entire candidate key of $R$:
     - $R_1 = (A, B, C)$ contains candidate key $\{A\}$ (and also $\{B, C\}$).
     - $R_2 = (C, D, E)$ contains candidate key $\{C, D\}$.
     - $R_4 = (E, A)$ contains candidate key $\{E\}$.
   - Since multiple candidate keys are already fully contained in the schema relations, **no additional candidate-key relation needs to be created**.

4. **Step 4: Redundancy Elimination (Subset Rule):**
   - Check if any relation schema $R_i$ is a proper subset of another relation schema $R_j$:
     - $R_1 = (A, B, C)$
     - $R_2 = (C, D, E)$
     - $R_3 = (B, D)$ — Notice that $R_3 \not\subseteq R_1$ (since $D \notin R_1$) and $R_3 \not\subseteq R_2$ (since $B \notin R_2$).
     - $R_4 = (E, A)$ — Not a subset of $R_1$ or $R_2$.
   - No relation is contained within another; all 4 relations must be retained.

5. **Final 3NF Decomposition:**
   $$
   \mathcal{D} = \{R_1(A, B, C), \; R_2(C, D, E), \; R_3(B, D), \; R_4(E, A)\}
   $$
   - **Lossless-Join:** Guaranteed because $R_1$ contains candidate key $\{A\}$.
   - **Dependency-Preserving:** Guaranteed because every FD in $F_c$ is directly represented within a relation.

**💡 RUET Exam Takeaway:**
Always write out the 4 formal steps of the 3NF synthesis algorithm. Never forget Step 3: if none of the generated relations contains a candidate key of $R$, you MUST add a new relation consisting solely of any candidate key!

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Remember the Cumulative Rule:** Always state "It must already be in 1NF" when defining 2NF, and "It must already be in 2NF" when defining 3NF. Normal forms are cumulative.
2. **The Single-Attribute Key Shortcut:** If a candidate key has only one attribute, partial dependencies are impossible. The relation is automatically in 2NF. Point this out in exam answers to demonstrate clear understanding.
3. **Do Not Confuse Partial and Transitive Dependencies:**
   - *Partial Dependency:* Part of key $\rightarrow$ non-prime attribute (violation of 2NF).
   - *Transitive Dependency:* Non-prime attribute $\rightarrow$ another non-prime attribute (violation of 3NF).
4. **Always Provide Concrete Minimal Tables:** When an exam question says "Explain with an example," never write only abstract symbols like $A \rightarrow B$. Give a simple 2-row table (such as `StudentID`, `CourseID`, `Grade`) with labeled primary keys.
5. **Keep Definitions Verbatim:** RUET examiners look for exact keywords: *atomic domains*, *partial dependency*, *proper subset of candidate key*, and *superkey or prime attribute*.
